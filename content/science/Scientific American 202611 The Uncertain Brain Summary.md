When the Eaton fire advanced toward Sierra Madre, California, in January 2025, neuroscientist Aura Harrison and research psychologist Galen Buckwalter faced uncertainty in its most dangerous form. Harrison had to decide when to evacuate despite having no evacuation order and little information about which choice offered the best chance of safety. Buckwalter, who uses a wheelchair, had no accessible vehicle and ultimately fled through smoke in his motorized chair. Both survived, and their experience became an unusually vivid test of the subject they had been studying: how brains make predictions in an unpredictable world, and why some people adapt while others become trapped by stress.

## The brain as a prediction engine

Brains do not wait passively for complete information. They combine sensory signals with memory to anticipate what is happening and what will happen next. When the prediction is wrong, the mismatch produces a "prediction error" that prompts learning or action. A gray shape on the floor becomes a cat until better visual information reveals a towel. In reward experiments, dopamine activity reflects the difference between an expected and an actual outcome, helping an animal revise its model of the situation.

Karl Friston's influential free energy principle extends this idea into a broad theory of brain function. On this account, organisms stay alive by using information to reduce uncertainty about their bodies and surroundings. Prediction is not merely a mental convenience. It helps regulate hunger, energy use, threat responses and behavior in a world where complete certainty is impossible.

The theory is powerful but not uncontested. Lisa Feldman Barrett argues that the brain's central task may be efficient energy management rather than minimizing surprise as such. The article treats predictive processing as a major framework, not a settled description of every neural function. Its most useful claim is narrower: unresolved uncertainty forces the brain to maintain competing possibilities, and doing so can be biologically expensive.

## When prediction errors do not end

A brief surprise can be helpful. A missed meal or sudden noise raises attention, releases stress hormones and encourages corrective action. Once the problem is solved, the response can subside, and the brain has learned something. Chronic uncertainty is different because the corrective loop never closes. Persistent activation of stress systems consumes energy and creates wear throughout the brain and body.

Glucocorticoids such as cortisol prepare the heart, lungs, muscles and brain for emergency action. If that emergency state persists, it can impair learning and memory, disrupt emotional control and increase the risk of physical illnesses including type 2 diabetes, heart attack and stroke. Early-life instability can be especially damaging because children need a broadly predictable environment in which to build reliable models of other people and the world.

Predictive processing also offers possible ways to understand depression and anxiety, although the article is careful about causation. Depression may involve retreating into a metaphorical "dark room," where avoidance reduces immediate surprise but also prevents the person from gathering new evidence. A bleak prediction can then become self-reinforcing because the brain gives more weight to confirming signals than to information that might update it. Anxiety can follow a related loop: avoiding every dog after one bite prevents the safe encounters that could correct the expectation of danger.

Intolerance of uncertainty is associated with generalized anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder and other conditions, but researchers do not yet know whether it is a cause, a symptom or both. Cognitive behavioral therapy can interrupt the loop by testing feared predictions in manageable steps. A child who gradually enters an anxiety-provoking situation and discovers that they can cope gains evidence that avoidance had concealed.

## Resilience means learning to update

Trauma does not produce the same outcome in everyone. Most people exposed to frightening events do not develop post-traumatic stress disorder, and early nightmares or intrusive memories may reflect the brain's attempt to integrate an extraordinary experience rather than the beginning of a chronic disorder. Prior trauma and weak social support raise risk, but researchers still struggle to predict who will recover quickly. This is the "resilience paradox."

The traits most consistently linked to resilience are flexible rather than fearless. People fare better when they can reinterpret arousal as readiness, retain confidence in their ability to cope, seek information and social support, and revise a plan when conditions change. Harrison, Buckwalter and Friston describe several possible paths through uncertainty, from fragile rigidity to a proactive habit of seeking useful information. These are not fixed personality types. Therapy, learning and repeated experience with manageable challenges can move people toward greater flexibility.

That point turns the article away from a promise of control. No technique can make fires, wars, disasters or political upheaval predictable. The practical goal is to become less dependent on any one forecast. Curiosity, gradual exposure to difficulty and a willingness to update expectations let the brain treat some surprise as information rather than only as threat. Resilience is not certainty restored. It is the capacity to keep learning when certainty is unavailable.
