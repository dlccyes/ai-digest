Social isolation is usually framed as a private injury: a person lacks close friends and suffers the consequences. Psychologists Angelica Puzio Ferrara and Dylan Vergara argue that this picture is incomplete. When a man's social world narrows, the women closest to him may become responsible for holding it together, often at a cost to their own time, autonomy and well-being.

The authors call this largely invisible labor "mankeeping." The name does not describe every relationship between men and women, nor does it identify a phenomenon that researchers have already measured directly. It is a theory assembled from several established patterns - men's comparatively fragile support networks, women's disproportionate share of relationship maintenance, and masculine norms that discourage emotional dependence on other men.

## When One Relationship Becomes the Whole Network

Social connection has weakened across many Western societies, but the decline is especially pronounced among men. Nearly two thirds of young American men report that no one really knows them well, and almost half of men in the United Kingdom say they cannot confide in friends about their problems. Large U.S. surveys also indicate that the share of people with no close friends has risen over the past 30 years, with a larger increase among men.

The difference is not simply that men have fewer contacts. Research on heterosexual couples suggests that men are more likely to treat a romantic partner as their primary source of intimate emotional support, whereas women more often turn to friends or relatives as well. The imbalance becomes visible after a partnership ends: men's social networks often contract, while women's tend to remain intact. Although much of the older evidence came from older white adults, more recent findings suggest that the broad pattern extends across ages and more diverse groups of straight men and women.

Cultural expectations help explain why. Ideals of masculinity in contemporary Western societies often reward emotional stoicism and extreme self-reliance. Men and boys who strongly endorse those ideals are less likely to form close friendships and tend to have worse mental and physical health. A man may therefore depend heavily on a female partner while also resisting the vulnerability required to build mutual support with male friends.

## The Labor That Keeps Men Connected

Women already perform more of the emotional and logistical work that sustains families: remembering birthdays, arranging gatherings and monitoring relatives' well-being. Sociologist Carolyn Rosenthal named this broader activity "kinkeeping" in the 1980s. Mankeeping is the authors' proposed term for the narrower work women do to compensate for men's frayed social ties - inviting a partner's friends over, encouraging him to contact them, and absorbing emotional needs that might otherwise be distributed across a community.

Several findings make the theory plausible. In research on Canadian couples, sociologist Barry Wellman found that women facilitated men's relationships with other men and consequently had less time for their own friendships. Other studies associate placing a partner's needs above one's own with psychological distress, relationship dissatisfaction and reduced personal autonomy among women. From this perspective, a woman's extra labor may be a rational attempt to protect her partner, her household and the relationship from the effects of his isolation, even as it imposes a personal cost.

The evidence does not yet establish mankeeping as a distinct, measurable phenomenon. The article presents no new experiment, sample or effect estimate testing the proposed chain. Existing studies document its components, but future research must determine where mankeeping is common, whether unequal emotional support actually predicts greater burden, and whether relationships with more reciprocal support produce better outcomes. The concept should therefore be treated as a research agenda, not a settled diagnosis or a universal claim about men.

## A Better Distribution of Support

Giving the pattern a name could make hidden labor easier to investigate and discuss, but naming it is only useful if responsibility does not remain with women. Programs such as Men's Circle and Beyond Equality offer men spaces to practice openness and mutual support with one another. The aim is not another exclusionary social club; it is a network in which emotional care is shared rather than concentrated in one romantic relationship.

That reframes men's isolation as more than a men's issue. Rigid masculine norms can leave men lonely while requiring women to repair the damage. Healthier friendships among men could widen their sources of care, reduce pressure on partners and make intimate relationships more reciprocal. The most important insight is systemic: when a social network collapses onto one person, isolation does not disappear. Its burden merely changes hands.
