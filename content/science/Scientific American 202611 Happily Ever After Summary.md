In early-1930s Budapest, a group of mathematically gifted students gathered to trade problems. Among them were Paul Erdős, George Szekeres and Esther Klein. One day Klein posed a question that looked almost too simple to matter: place five points anywhere on a page, provided no three lie on one straight line. Must four of them form a convex quadrilateral, a four-sided shape with no inward dent or crossed edge?

The answer is always yes. That small puzzle became the starting point for the Erdős-Szekeres happy-ending problem, helped open a path into modern Ramsey theory, and brought Klein and Szekeres together for life.

## Why Five Points Are Enough

Klein's proof begins with a rubber-band picture. Imagine that the five points are pegs and stretch a band around the outermost ones. If the band touches four pegs, those four already form the required convex quadrilateral. If it touches all five, removing any one leaves four points on a convex boundary. The only difficult case is when the band touches three pegs, making a triangle, while the other two points sit inside it.

Draw a line through the two interior points. Of the triangle's three corners, at least two must lie on the same side of that line. Those two outer corners, together with the two interior points, form a convex quadrilateral. None of the four can be trapped inside a triangle formed by the other three: the outer corners are too exposed, and the line through the inner pair keeps them in the necessary order. What can fail with four arbitrary points becomes unavoidable when a fifth is added.

Klein then asked the question that turns a pleasant puzzle into a research program. How many points are needed to guarantee a convex pentagon, hexagon or polygon with any chosen number of sides? Perhaps clever arrangements could avoid large convex polygons forever.

Erdős and Szekeres proved in 1935 that they cannot. For every desired number of sides, some finite collection of points, with no three collinear, is large enough that a convex polygon of that size must appear. Their theorem guaranteed existence, even when it did not reveal the smallest number of points required.

## Order Hiding inside Disorder

That result shares the central idea of Ramsey theory: a sufficiently large system cannot remain completely disordered. Frank Ramsey had proved an abstract version using a party. For any chosen group size, a sufficiently large party must contain that many guests who are either pairwise acquaintances or pairwise strangers. A host can arrange individual relationships however they like, but beyond a certain size a coherent pattern becomes inevitable.

The geometric problem says something similar about points. They may be scattered without any obvious plan, yet enough of them force a neatly ordered convex subset. Erdős became a central figure in Ramsey theory, and the happy-ending problem helped draw attention to this broader search for structure that no sufficiently large arrangement can avoid.

The frustrating part is quantitative. Proving that order eventually appears is often easier than finding the exact threshold. Five points guarantee a convex quadrilateral. Nine guarantee a convex pentagon, while eight can avoid one. The next case resisted for about 70 years. In work published after Szekeres's death, he and mathematical physicist Lindsay Peters used an extensive computer search to establish that 17 points guarantee a convex hexagon, whereas 16 can still avoid one.

Erdős and Szekeres conjectured a general formula: the minimum number needed for a convex polygon with *n* sides is two raised to the power (*n* minus two), plus one. The known quadrilateral, pentagon and hexagon cases fit it, but the formula has not been proved for every larger polygon. Erdős left a \\$500 prize for a proof. Andrew Suk came tantalizingly close in 2016, but the prize remains unclaimed.

## The Other Happy Ending

Erdős gave the problem its name because Klein and Szekeres fell in love while the group worked through the geometry. They married in 1937. As a Jewish couple confronting Nazi persecution, they fled Europe and spent the war years in Shanghai, where their first child was born. They resettled in Australia in 1948. Szekeres became a professor and later president of the Australian Mathematical Society; Klein taught mathematics and continued posing problems to students.

Both died in 2005, within an hour of each other. Their personal story gives the problem its memorable title, but its mathematical legacy is larger. A handful of dots revealed a durable principle: randomness and freedom of arrangement have limits. Once a system becomes large enough, some pattern is no longer merely likely. It is forced.
