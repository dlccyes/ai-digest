Gravitational waves let astronomers listen to cosmic violence that may produce little or no light. These waves are minute distortions of spacetime, set off when massive objects move dramatically. ## A signal written in spacetime

Einstein's general theory of relativity says that mass shapes the space around it. In 1916, soon after publishing that theory, he predicted that accelerating masses could send ripples through spacetime. Black holes and neutron stars create especially strong waves when they orbit one another and collide, making events millions or billions of light-years away measurable on Earth.

A binary black-hole system illustrates the process. As the two objects circle, they radiate energy in gravitational waves. Their orbit shrinks, the circling accelerates, and the holes finally merge. The resulting, more massive black hole then settles into a stable state while the distortions fade. The outgoing waves preserve characteristic frequencies and amplitudes from that sequence, turning an otherwise dark collision into a detectable signal.

The detections come from three observatories working as a global network: the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory, or LIGO, in the U.S.; Virgo in Italy; and KAGRA in Japan. LIGO made the first direct detection in 2015, nearly a century after Einstein's prediction.

## A ruler sensitive to almost nothing

LIGO measures distances with two four-kilometer tunnels arranged at right angles. A laser is split so that one beam travels down each arm. Mirrors send the beams back and forth several times before they recombine at a detector. When the apparatus is undisturbed, both beams follow equal paths and cancel each other when they meet.

A passing gravitational wave stretches space in one direction while compressing it in the other. That changes the beams' travel distances by an extraordinarily small amount. They no longer return in perfect alignment, so instead of canceling they form a distinct interference pattern. The instrument does not photograph the wave; it converts a tiny difference in path length into a measurable change in light.

This method gives scientists a channel for observing events that ordinary telescopes may miss. It also turns extreme objects into tests of gravity. The growing catalog shows that gravitational-wave astronomy has moved beyond a single landmark detection to a population of events with unusual masses and spins. Researchers can compare those signals with the shapes predicted by general relativity, looking for discrepancies that might point toward a deeper theory.
