Psilocybin can make the boundary between self and world feel permeable or disappear altogether. A new brain-imaging study suggests that this sense of “oneness” is not simply the product of neural chaos. Even as the drug disrupts familiar communication patterns, it may reveal a different kind of order—one that becomes strongest when people feel least separate from their surroundings.

## Order inside a disordered brain

During a psilocybin trip, networks of neurons that ordinarily work together can become desynchronized. This loosening of established connections may help the brain form new ones and could contribute to psilocybin’s potential value for some mental health conditions. But it creates a puzzle: if activity becomes more random, why do many people describe their trips as unusually coherent, meaningful experiences?

To investigate, researchers gave psilocybin to 62 healthy adults who had not previously used psychedelics. Functional magnetic resonance imaging recorded their brain activity while they rested, meditated, listened to music and watched a movie. Many participants reported “embeddedness,” a blissful feeling of merging with the scanner, the room or something larger than themselves.

The scans showed a broad shift in the balance of information processing. Networks that handle sensory input from the outside world became less connected with the rest of the brain, while networks involved in higher-level processing became more connected. The result is consistent with experience becoming less firmly anchored to incoming sights and sounds and more shaped by internally generated activity.

The researchers then used machine learning to compare how each person’s activity changed over time and across tasks. Among people whose subjective experiences were less intense, the patterns varied widely, even when they were doing the same thing. Among those who reported a strong loss of the self–world boundary, however, important dimensions of brain activity converged. Music, meditation, rest and movie watching each produced a more recognizable group pattern. The more alike participants felt to the world around them, the more alike their network activity became.

That convergence is the study’s “hidden order.” Psilocybin did not simply make every brain uniformly organized. Instead, a shared structure emerged most clearly in the people whose ordinary sense of an independent self had been most altered.

## Why setting matters

The finding may help explain why the setting of a psychedelic experience has so much influence over its course. If different activities evoke distinct and consistent activity patterns during profound trips, then music, surroundings and a person’s focus are not incidental details. They can help shape the experience itself.

That sensitivity is both useful and demanding. A controlled, reassuring environment might support a constructive therapeutic session, but it would also make treatment more expensive and difficult to deliver. The study points toward more individualized research as well. Following one person’s changing brain patterns throughout a trip may clarify why the same drug produces blissful unity for one person and a very different experience for another.

The results do not settle the central clinical question. Researchers still disagree over whether any therapeutic benefit comes from the conscious experience of tripping or from changes in connectivity that might occur underneath it. This study links reported experience to network organization, but it does not show which causes the other or demonstrate that the observed patterns produce lasting improvement.

## The self as an active construction

The philosophical implication is as striking as the neurological one. Everyday consciousness presents the self and the outside world as naturally separate. The study suggests that the brain must continually maintain that division. Psilocybin may not manufacture an illusion of unity so much as temporarily weaken the processes that usually keep experience partitioned into “me” and “everything else.”

That does not prove that psychedelic oneness is a truer picture of reality. It supports the view that the familiar boundary between self and world is actively maintained rather than simply fixed. By pairing first-person reports with changing brain patterns, the research turns an elusive feature of consciousness into something scientists can begin to measure—without pretending that the mystery has been solved.
