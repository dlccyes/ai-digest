## Happiness in Minutes, Not Months

Programs meant to improve well-being often ask for substantial commitment: daily meditation, long conversations or weeks of gratitude journaling. Those practices can help, but they are hard to maintain when people are already tired or overwhelmed. “Small Acts of Joy Bring Big Gains,” by psychologists Darwin A. Guevarra and Emiliana Simon-Thomas and biomedical informatics researcher Xuhai “Orson” Xu, asks whether much smaller actions can make a meaningful difference.

Their answer comes from the Big Joy Project, a free online community-science program inspired by the film *Mission: Joy—Finding Happiness in Troubled Times*. Participants receive prompts for activities that take roughly five to 10 minutes. One asks them to make a gratitude list; others invite an act of kindness, reflection on a core value, loving-kindness toward someone else, reframing a difficult experience, celebrating another person’s good news or watching an awe-inspiring video. The opening exercise is simpler still: 42 seconds of people laughing.

The project treats these exercises as “micro acts”—brief opportunities to direct attention toward gratitude, connection, meaning or wonder. Participants rate their positive and negative feelings before and after each activity and can record reflections during an evening check-in. More than 100,000 people in over 200 countries and territories have joined and completed more than 400,000 micro acts, making the project unusually large and geographically broad for research on everyday joy.

## What Changed after One Week

The researchers analyzed responses from 17,598 participants in 169 countries and territories. They compared answers to 18 questions asked before the program with answers to most of the same questions after seven days. Participants reported greater emotional well-being and more positive emotion, less stress, and modest improvements in sleep quality and physical health.

The apparent benefit rose with participation: people who completed more micro acts generally reported larger gains in happiness. The project was also associated with increased prosociality, the inclination to help others and engage with a community. Here too, completing more activities was linked to greater improvement.

The pattern did not appear to be confined to people with abundant time or resources. Participants reporting greater financial strain, less education or lower subjective social status often showed larger increases in well-being. Growth in prosociality was especially pronounced among men, who began with lower scores on that measure, as well as among people with lower socioeconomic status and people living in the Global South.

These findings fit a plausible psychological account. Gratitude and awe can interrupt habitual attention to problems; kindness and shared celebration reinforce social connection; reflection on values can restore a sense of meaning. The activities may also strengthen agency by showing that a person can deliberately influence a moment of experience instead of waiting for a vacation, promotion or other major event to produce happiness.

## Encouraging Evidence, Not a Controlled Test

The scale of the project is impressive, but its design sharply limits what can be concluded. Participants chose to join a program explicitly devoted to joy, reported their own feelings and knew what the activities were intended to accomplish. Most important, the analysis did not include a control group that completed no micro acts. It therefore cannot separate the exercises’ effects from expectation, the passage of time, repeated measurement or differences between people who completed more activities and those who completed fewer.

The dose-response pattern is suggestive rather than causal for the same reason. Happier, healthier or more motivated participants may simply have found it easier to finish more prompts. Self-reported sleep and physical health also do not establish objective biological changes. The authors acknowledge these weaknesses and call for future studies that use other kinds of measurement and compare participants with a no-intervention group.

The project’s strongest lesson is therefore practical but modest. Brief, intentional acts of gratitude, kindness, connection and awe are accessible ways to make room for positive experience, and thousands of participants reported feeling better after practicing them for a week. Whether the effects last, how much the exercises themselves cause the change and which activities help which people remain open questions. Happiness may not require a lifestyle overhaul, but proving the value of its smallest ingredients still requires a more rigorous experiment.
