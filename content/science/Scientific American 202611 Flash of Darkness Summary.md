## A strange collision in liquid xenon

LZ contains seven metric tons of extremely cold liquid xenon in a former gold mine about a mile underground. The rock above shields the detector from much of the cosmic radiation that could imitate a rare particle interaction. When something strikes a xenon atom, the collision can produce both a burst of light and freed electrons. Their relative sizes and timing help researchers reconstruct what happened.

The 2023 event produced the combination expected when an unknown particle collides with a xenon nucleus. It also appeared far removed from the ordinary radioactive, electronic and detector effects that make up LZ's known backgrounds. After roughly four decades in which direct-detection experiments have found no persuasive dark-matter particle, even a single unexplained nuclear recoil deserves attention.

The statistical evidence remains modest by particle-physics standards. The collaboration estimated about a 0.5 percent chance that background alone would produce a result at least this signal-like. A confirmed discovery in the field usually requires a chance probability of roughly 0.00003 percent, and one unusual event cannot establish a new particle regardless of how poorly it matches known noise. At the time of the article, the result had been presented at the TeVPA conference and submitted to *Physical Review Letters* but had not completed journal review.

The analysis also lacked a crucial protection against wishful thinking. Researchers examined the first 220 days of data without first hiding, or “blinding,” the region in which a signal might appear. Blinding prevents analysts from unconsciously tuning their methods around an exciting result. The team therefore presents the collision as a candidate to investigate, not as a dark-matter detection.

## Why it does not fit the usual WIMP story

Astronomers infer dark matter from its gravity: visible stars and gas move as though the universe contains about five times more invisible matter than ordinary matter. One long-standing candidate is the weakly interacting massive particle, or WIMP. A WIMP would normally pass through matter untouched but could occasionally hit an atomic nucleus, which is exactly the kind of recoil LZ is built to register.

The puzzle is the event's energy. Standard WIMP models predict that the lower-energy collisions should be more common. If a conventional WIMP produced LZ's unusually forceful recoil, the detector should already have recorded dozens or hundreds of gentler ones. It has not. Making the event fit dark matter would therefore require a less familiar particle interaction or a mechanism that accelerated a WIMP to an unexpectedly high speed.

That mismatch cuts both ways. It leaves room for physics beyond the simplest models, but it also raises the possibility of an unrecognized background. Dark matter is not the only unknown in an instrument sensitive enough to notice extraordinarily rare processes.

## A decisive test is already waiting

LZ has accumulated more than 700 additional days of properly blinded data that await full analysis. If that larger sample contains more high-energy events with the same pattern, the original flash will become much harder to dismiss. If it contains none, the event will look more like an anomaly. Independent xenon detectors, including PandaX-4T and XENONnT, can then check whether any repeated signal is unique to LZ or appears across experiments.

The timing matters because the current style of WIMP search is approaching a natural limit. As detectors grow larger and more sensitive, neutrinos from the sun and Earth's atmosphere will increasingly produce nuclear recoils that resemble dark-matter interactions. Physicist Juan Collar estimates that within roughly the next decade this “neutrino fog” could make conventional WIMP searches much harder and force researchers toward different strategies.

LZ's flash may be the first glimpse of an unexpected dark-matter particle, an unfamiliar detector background or a singular statistical accident.
