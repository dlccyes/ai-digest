Conscious attention does not capture everything the brain learns. A study described by Nora Bradford in “Wandering Mind” found that people could pick up hidden regularities in a simple task while their thoughts were elsewhere. During periods of reported mind wandering, they adapted to those patterns faster.

Péter Simor of Eötvös Loránd University in Budapest and his colleagues asked participants to press keys matching the directions of arrows on a screen. The task contained probabilistic patterns: regularities that could improve performance without being consciously recognized. Participants learned them without realizing they were there. Their reports of drifting attention coincided with significantly faster learning of the hidden structure.

The simplicity of the task matters. Previous research often examined mind wandering during prolonged, demanding activities, where losing focus typically hurts performance. This experiment instead asked what happens when an activity requires little attention, leaving room for thoughts unrelated to the immediate task.

## A possible connection to sleep

Electroencephalogram recordings, which measure the brain's electrical activity, showed more slow waves during the mind-wandering periods. These waves are prominent during sleep. The researchers proposed that wandering attention might share some features of light sleep, potentially including benefits for learning. At the time of the article, they planned to investigate narcolepsy and sleep deprivation to explore whether the effect could relate to lost sleep. The experiment did not establish that daydreaming substitutes for sleep.

The causal explanation also remains open. Psychologist Jonathan Smallwood, who was not involved in the study, cautioned that mind wandering and improved learning might both arise from the same underlying state. Their occurring together does not show that deliberately letting attention drift would produce the benefit. The short report gives neither the sample size nor the numerical size of the improvement, limiting how precisely readers can assess its strength.

The finding offers a narrow but revealing insight into conscious and unconscious processing: a person can be mentally occupied elsewhere while ongoing activity still teaches the brain something. Learning can continue outside the focus of awareness, even though attention remains important when tasks become difficult.

*Source: Nora Bradford, “Wandering Mind,” Scientific American, July/August 2025, Advances.*
