Quantum mechanics works with extraordinary precision, yet its standard textbook rules tell two incompatible stories. An isolated system evolves smoothly as a wave of possibilities, but a measurement supposedly makes that wave collapse to one outcome. The Many Worlds theory removes the collapse rule. Its price is enormous: every outcome remains real in a different branch of a universal wave function.

Recent experiments do not prove that this multiverse exists. They do, however, keep extending quantum behavior into larger systems and across greater distances, weakening the old intuition that quantum rules must fail somewhere between an atom and an observer.

## The measurement problem

Schrödinger’s equation describes a quantum system with a wave function that can combine several possible states in a superposition. Like overlapping ripples on a pond, those states interfere: crests can reinforce one another, while crests and troughs can cancel. Crucially, the equation contains no mechanism that destroys a superposition.

The measurement postulate says something different. If an atom is in a superposition of two positions, a detector records only one, chosen probabilistically. The wave function appears to collapse. But the detector and experimenter are themselves made of atoms. Applying Schrödinger’s equation to the whole laboratory should therefore leave the atom, detector and observer together in a superposition of both results.

This is the measurement problem. Standard quantum mechanics uses one rule for the object being measured and another for the measuring apparatus, without specifying exactly where the boundary lies.

In 1957 Hugh Everett III proposed eliminating the collapse rule and treating Schrödinger’s equation as universal. A measurement would then entangle the observer with every possible result. One branch contains an experimenter who saw the atom at the first position; another contains an otherwise identical experimenter who saw it at the second. As microscopic uncertainty spreads into macroscopic events, the universal wave function continually develops branches. What is usually called an “interpretation” is therefore a substantive physical claim: the other outcomes do not disappear but persist as other worlds.

## Why one world still looks classical

If all branches remain, why can no one interact with alternate outcomes? The answer offered by the theory is decoherence. A macroscopic object contains roughly 10<sup>23</sup> particles interacting with one another and with their surroundings. Their quantum waves acquire complicated, effectively uncontrolled relationships. Constructive interference among entire macroscopic branches becomes vanishingly difficult to observe, while decoherence suppresses observable interference between them.

Decoherence is not an added collapse law. It follows from ordinary quantum evolution when many components become entangled. Each decohered branch can consequently behave like an independent classical world even though the full wave function still contains all of them. In the article’s analogy, the multiverse resembles separate rays of light crossing a dark space: the darkness between them is produced by destructive interference, not by an absence of waves.

This explanation also clarifies why quantum computers are so fragile. Keeping even dozens of particles in a controlled superposition requires extreme isolation and precision. Unmanaged interactions with the environment rapidly hide the interference on which a computation depends.

## Evidence without a decisive test

Experiments have repeatedly pushed the apparent quantum–classical boundary outward. Double-slit interference has been demonstrated with molecules containing about 2,000 atoms. Clouds of thousands of ultracold atoms form Bose–Einstein condensates, and superconducting devices show quantum behavior across centimeter scales. Quantum optical states have also been transmitted between South Africa and China over more than 12,900 kilometers, although that result does not mean an object of that size occupied a superposition.

Together, these results give less room to proposals in which Schrödinger’s equation simply stops working above some microscopic scale. They support the universal equation assumed by Many Worlds, but they do not distinguish it cleanly from every rival account. No experiment has directly revealed another branch, and there is still no agreed perfect test of whether collapse truly occurs.

Other arguments are suggestive rather than conclusive. Cosmological inflation applies quantum physics to a field spanning the early universe. The apparent fine-tuning of physical constants can be paired with an anthropic argument in which observers necessarily find themselves in a life-compatible universe. Both ideas fit naturally with a multiplicity of worlds, but neither requires Everett’s theory by itself.

A major unresolved problem lies inside the theory. If every possible outcome occurs, what does it mean to assign probabilities to them? Advocates still disagree over how the familiar quantum probabilities emerge and why an observer should expect one branch rather than another. Decoherence explains why branches cease to interfere in practice; it does not by itself explain chance.

Many Worlds replaces an unexplained collapse with a single universal rule, but it trades one mystery for a vast and difficult ontology. The growing scale of quantum experiments makes that trade harder to dismiss as mere science fiction. It does not yet make the multiverse an established fact.
