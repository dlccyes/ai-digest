## A collapse story that collapses under scrutiny

Rapa Nui, often called Easter Island, has long been presented as a warning for the whole planet. In the familiar version, its Polynesian settlers multiplied beyond the island's limits, felled the last trees, exhausted the soil and squandered labor on hundreds of enormous stone statues. Hunger and warfare followed. By the time Europeans arrived, the society had supposedly destroyed itself.

The island that British surgeon J. Linton Palmer encountered in 1868 certainly looked devastated. Little more than 100 Rapanui remained, and many of the statues, or *moai*, had been toppled. But Michael Marshall's account shows how later observers worked backward from that ruin and blamed the islanders. Evidence from archaeology, ancient DNA, satellite mapping and historical records increasingly points elsewhere: Rapanui society adapted to a difficult environment for centuries, while its catastrophic population loss followed sustained contact with outsiders.

Polynesian voyagers permanently settled Rapa Nui around 1200 C.E., although some researchers argue for earlier intermittent visits. The isolated, subtropical island had erratic rain, limited freshwater and no large hardwood trees for oceangoing canoes. Even so, the first detailed European account complicates the image of a society already collapsing. Dutch sailor Jacob Roggeveen described cultivated land and well-fed people in 1722. His landing party also shot and killed 10 to 12 Rapanui, foreshadowing the violence that would come from abroad.

## Adaptation, not blind destruction

Researchers have confirmed that Rapa Nui was once forested and that its native palm disappeared. People cleared land, and their early slash-and-burn methods were poorly suited to palms that could take about 70 years to bear fruit. But humans were not the only force. Polynesian rats arrived with the settlers, multiplied rapidly and ate palm nuts before the seeds could germinate. Hundreds of rat bones appear in some of the earliest deposits, and preserved nuts bear gnaw marks. Human clearing, slow tree growth and an exploding rat population together made forest recovery unlikely.

When the trees dwindled, Rapanui farmers changed their methods. They covered fields with stones in a practice called lithic mulching. Weathering rock released phosphorus and potassium, while the stones held soil in place and reduced evaporation. Excavations also show that people deliberately planted palms near ceremonial architecture. At Ava Ranga Uka a Toroke Hau, researchers uncovered walls, dams, basins and underground channels that managed the island's only seasonal stream. The system may have helped people endure a drought that began around 1550.

A 2024 satellite and machine-learning survey estimated that rock gardens covered much less land than earlier studies had suggested. If correct, they could have fed only a few thousand people, undermining claims that a huge population boomed and crashed. The conclusion remains debated because remote sensing may miss upland cultivation and faint garden traces. Even with that uncertainty, the agricultural record describes planning under scarcity rather than heedless ruin.

The *moai* also look less like ruinously expensive monuments once researchers test how they moved. Statues left along ancient roads lean forward, allowing teams to rock them from side to side with ropes and effectively "walk" them. In one experiment, 18 people moved a 4.35-ton replica 100 meters in 40 minutes. Transporting roughly 600 statues beyond the quarry did not necessarily require vast timber works or a crushing labor force; their design appears to have made motion possible.

## The catastrophe came by ship

If a violent pre-European collapse occurred, it should have left recognizable traces. Researchers have found no island-wide fortifications, mass graves or widespread weaponry, and little evidence of bones damaged by large-scale combat. Conflict almost certainly occurred, as it did elsewhere in Polynesia, but that is not proof that war destroyed the society.

Ancient DNA supplies another test. A 2024 study sequenced remains from 15 people who lived between about 1670 and 1950. Their genomes did not show the loss of genetic diversity expected after a severe precontact population crash. Modeling instead suggested centuries of slow growth in a population that never became especially large. The DNA also contained Native American ancestry dating to roughly 1250 to 1430, evidence of trans-Pacific contact generations before Europeans entered the region. The route and participants remain uncertain, but Rapa Nui was not simply a sealed-off dead end.

The island's undisputed demographic disaster followed European arrival. Foreign ships introduced smallpox, tuberculosis and sexually transmitted infections. In 1862 and 1863, Peruvian raiders kidnapped Rapanui as forced laborers, taking knowledge holders as well as lives. Chile annexed the island in 1888, and commercial sheep ranching later stripped vegetation while the surviving Rapanui were confined to Hanga Roa and needed permission to leave. Researchers mistook a landscape damaged by disease, slavery, colonial rule and ranching for the remains of an Indigenous environmental suicide.

Recent history reinforces the correction. When the COVID-19 pandemic stopped regular flights and tourism for nearly three years, islanders expanded farming, revived rock gardens, managed water and erosion, and traded fish for crops. The response echoed the practical resilience of their ancestors. Rapa Nui now faces sea-level rise that could threaten Ahu Tongariki and its 15 standing *moai*—another danger largely generated elsewhere.

The revised story does not make Rapa Nui a lost paradise. Life there was difficult, resources were limited, forests vanished and conflict occurred. But the strongest evidence describes endurance followed by externally imposed catastrophe. The island's real warning is how readily outsiders can turn damage they caused into a morality tale about the people who survived it.
