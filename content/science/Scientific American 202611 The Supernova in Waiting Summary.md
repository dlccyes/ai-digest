Betelgeuse is famous partly because it seems poised for catastrophe. The red supergiant, which marks Orion's right shoulder, has more than a dozen times the sun's mass and shines tens of thousands of times more brightly. It will eventually explode as a supernova. Yet even this heavily studied nearby star keeps surprising astronomers, and a newly supported companion star makes its future more interesting than the usual question of when it will blow up.

## A hidden star in the glare

Betelgeuse has several overlapping rhythms. Its brightness varies on a roughly 400-day cycle, but it also shows a puzzling six-year fluctuation. Astronomers suspected that the longer cycle might come from a second star passing behind the swollen supergiant. Observations from 2024 hinted at such an object, and a newer paper provides stronger evidence for a luminous companion about three times the mass of the sun.

The likely companion, provisionally called Betelgeuse B, remained hidden because Betelgeuse A is overwhelmingly brighter. Researchers can test the interpretation in 2027: after half an orbit, the candidate should appear on the opposite side of Betelgeuse A from where it was detected in 2024. Its minimum orbital distance is about 1.3 billion kilometers, comparable to Saturn's distance from the sun. That sounds spacious, but Betelgeuse A is so distended that it would fill half the sky as seen from the companion.

The proposed companion adds to a long list of Betelgeuse mysteries. Ancient Chinese astronomers described the star as yellow, raising questions about whether its color and structure have changed over two millennia. Its dramatic dimming in 2019 and 2020 was eventually traced to a huge cloud of dust expelled by the star, not to an imminent explosion. Even its distance from Earth remains uncertain.

## The explosion is certain; its date is not

Betelgeuse A could technically explode at any time, but the available evidence points to a wait closer to 100,000 years. Its core is now fusing helium into carbon. As the star evolves, progressively heavier nuclei will form until iron accumulates. Iron fusion consumes rather than releases energy, removing the pressure support that holds up the star. The core will collapse, temperatures will soar toward 100 billion degrees Celsius, and the star will erupt as a supernova.

The blast would release within moments as much energy as the sun will produce over its entire lifetime. Despite the spectacle, astronomers expect the effects on Earth to be modest because Betelgeuse is far away. Betelgeuse B, however, would be sitting almost beside the explosion.

Phil Plait emphasizes that little research has directly modeled what happens to a companion in a wide binary when the other star explodes, so he uses explicitly rough, back-of-the-envelope calculations. The best evidence that Betelgeuse B can survive comes from runaway stars. Some stars racing through the galaxy at hundreds of kilometers per second were probably once companions to stars that went supernova. When an exploding primary suddenly loses much of its mass, its gravitational hold weakens and the companion can be flung into space. Because some known survivors likely orbited even closer than Betelgeuse B does, the candidate companion is unlikely to be destroyed.

Survival would not mean escaping untouched. The initial heat pulse would be enormous but brief. Ejected stellar matter traveling at thousands of kilometers per second would strike the companion, heat it and probably strip away some of its outer material. The debris would spread and thin rapidly, however, and the fraction that actually hit Betelgeuse B would be tiny compared with the companion's own mass.

Visible light and fast-moving debris account for only a small share of the supernova's energy. Roughly 99 percent would leave as an estimated 10<sup>58</sup> neutrinos. These particles usually pass through ordinary matter, but the torrent at Betelgeuse B's distance would be so intense that a human there would absorb a nearly fatal neutrino dose. A star is a different scale of object: the absorbed neutrino energy would still be negligible beside Betelgeuse B's own output.

Betelgeuse A would probably leave behind an ultradense, strongly magnetized neutron star. Exactly how that remnant, the sudden mass loss and the blast would reshape the surviving system depends on details that astronomers cannot yet predict. That uncertainty is the article's larger point. Betelgeuse is bright, nearby and observed obsessively, yet researchers still do not know its precise distance, the full nature of its variability or the fate of its possible companion. The star is compelling not because its explosion is around the corner, but because even an apparently familiar object can keep the mechanics of its life and death hidden.
