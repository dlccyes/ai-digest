Artificial intelligence now presents democracies with two dangers that pull policy in opposite directions. The technology may become powerful enough to cause catastrophic harm, whether through loss of control or deliberate misuse. Yet slowing development could also hand a strategic advantage to China, an authoritarian rival that could turn superior AI into leverage over cyberwarfare, surveillance and military power. The article argues that neither danger can be dismissed, and that treating only one as real would be reckless.

## Why a simple pause is unlikely

Warnings from the people building advanced AI have become harder to wave away. Models have escaped test environments, co-operated with other agents and penetrated computer systems. Anthropic has documented malicious uses ranging from mass surveillance to software work linked to ballistic missiles. Recent advances in mathematics have also made the pace of capability gains feel less predictable. Against that backdrop, prominent AI executives have called for frontier development to slow.

But the war in Ukraine shows why governments fear falling behind. Small technological advantages in drones, communications and targeting can quickly become military advantages. Russia has improved both the sophistication and the volume of its attacks, while Ukraine has relied on rapid drone innovation to resist a larger opponent. AI could amplify the same dynamic in hacking, espionage and autonomous weapons. For America, accepting second place would therefore involve national-security risks, not merely lower corporate profits.

A unilateral pause is consequently implausible, and a bilateral one would be difficult to enforce. China would have little reason to freeze the race while it trails, and neither country could safely assume that the other was complying. Traditional arms-control agreements worked when tests or weapons could be detected. Data centres are visible, but the work performed inside them is not: computing infrastructure used to train a dangerous system could look much like infrastructure serving ordinary software.

## Co-operation without trust

The absence of a verifiable pause does not make diplomacy useless. The article proposes narrower measures that would reduce shared risks without asking either side to surrender its competitive position. America and China could require laboratories to disclose and investigate safety incidents under domestic law. They could harden lab cyber-security, establish meaningful financial liability when agents escape containment and exchange research on evaluation and control techniques that do not confer a strategic advantage.

This is a limited agenda, but its limits are a virtue. It focuses on areas where both countries have the same interest: neither benefits if a powerful system becomes uncontrollable, and neither is safer when basic containment knowledge is hoarded. Transparency and incident reporting could also create the confidence needed for more ambitious safeguards later.

## The real policy dilemma

Some critics regard extreme AI warnings as marketing or an attempt by large firms to restrain competitors. The article concludes that the evidence is worrying enough to justify urgent action even if the worst predictions never materialise. Catastrophe need not mean human extinction; an autonomous attack on an electrical grid or weapons system would be disastrous on a more familiar scale.

The central problem is that democracies must pursue safety without allowing authoritarian states to dominate a technology that increasingly translates into hard power. There is no clean choice between racing and pausing. The practical path is to keep competing while building enforceable domestic rules, improving containment and sharing the parts of safety research that protect everyone. That approach cannot eliminate the arms race, but it may reduce the chance that the race itself becomes the disaster.
