## A battlefield without a rear

Russia has gained little ground in 2026, and its casualties may now exceed its ability to recruit replacements. Along the front, Ukraine's forces have also become more capable and less dependent on inherited Soviet methods. Yet the apparent stalemate hides constant adaptation. The traditional front line has spread into a contested band 40-80km deep, where first-person-view drones hunt soldiers, vehicles and supply routes. Some drones wait in ambush, while unmanned ground vehicles carry food and ammunition through areas where ordinary movement has become perilous.

Both sides are extending that contest farther into the rear. Ukraine has used long-range drones against Russian logistics and oil infrastructure and has tried to isolate occupied Crimea. Russia is developing its own network of aerial relays, allowing piloted drones to pursue moving targets as far as roughly 200km inside Ukraine. Trains that once faced danger mainly while stopped with military cargo are now attacked in motion, including passenger services.

The greater imbalance appears at still longer ranges. Russia is replacing many propeller-driven Shahed drones with faster jet-powered versions, reportedly using engines imported from China. Ukraine's machine guns and shoulder-fired missiles struggle to reach them, while its ageing fighter aircraft lack ammunition and frequently require maintenance abroad. New Ukrainian interceptors are being tested, but scaling them will take money and time. Russia, meanwhile, is aiming to produce 24,000 jet Shaheds in 2026 and had reportedly used only about 1,000 by the time of the article.

Ballistic missiles pose an even harder problem. Russia may now be able to launch more than 100 of all types each month, while Ukraine has too few Patriot interceptors. Building a domestic equivalent requires not just a missile but sophisticated radar, communications and guidance systems. Ukraine's defence industry is advancing quickly, but this is a project measured in years, not months.

## Economic attrition becomes a weapon

For much of the war, air defences and resilient businesses allowed Ukraine's large cities to function. Tax revenue, exports and everyday routines supported both the state and public morale. Russia's latest campaign is attacking that foundation directly.

Strikes have shut major steel plants in Kryvyi Rih and Zaporizhia, pushing August output 60% below the level a year earlier. Warehouses, supermarkets, e-commerce networks, postal facilities, petrol stations and railways have all been hit. More than 250 locomotives were destroyed in 2026, according to the article. Repairs have kept trains moving, but repeated attacks could strain the network severely.

The effective closure of Odessa's deep-water ports may be even more damaging. Agriculture generated more than a tenth of Ukraine's GDP through exports the previous year. If crops cannot leave the country, domestic prices will fall, storage will fill and large producers may decide not to plant the next season. Russia can therefore weaken future output without capturing the farmland itself.

Winter magnifies every vulnerability. Ukraine has hardened parts of its energy system, but Kyiv and other large cities still depend on central heating plants and a small number of electrical choke points. Damage to one major substation west of Kyiv could reduce the capital from roughly 16 hours of electricity a day to four. Prolonged outages would threaten heating, water, food distribution and sewage systems, while repeated air-raid alerts already interrupt schools, transport and businesses.

Families who leave make the economy smaller still. Ukraine has already lost roughly a million people to military service and a similar number to draft avoidance. Every additional departure shrinks the labour force and tax base at the moment when military spending is rising.

## The financial front

Ukraine's growing military-technology sector offers one bright spot. Nearly half of its 20 largest taxpayers are now defence firms, many operating through decentralised facilities that are difficult to target. In time, they could become major exporters. For now, however, they cannot offset the loss of industrial capacity, trade and ordinary commerce.

The first weeks of Russia's intensified campaign were expected to cost the government 70bn hryvnia, or about \\$1.6bn, in tax revenue. Kyiv had already asked European partners to cover a \\$27bn gap in the military budget, and analysts raised their forecast for the year's overall deficit. The finance ministry estimated that Ukraine would need another \\$32.6bn in foreign financing in 2027 beyond what had already been promised.

Europe is providing large loans, but some funds depend on reforms that Ukraine's increasingly contentious parliament has not yet passed. This makes political cohesion part of the battlefield. Russia does not need to defeat Ukraine's army outright if it can exhaust the economy, disrupt city life and use winter hardship to deepen public anger.

The article rejects both easy optimism and imminent-defeat fatalism. Ukraine remains capable of inflicting extraordinary losses and denying Russia a decisive advance. But tactical success at the front does not protect the railways, ports, power system and public finances that make resistance sustainable. The next phase of the war will depend as much on interceptors, electricity and European financing as on territory. Ukraine still has the will to endure; its partners must help ensure that it retains the means.
