Starlink began as a way to deliver broadband from orbit. SpaceX now wants to turn it into a full mobile-phone network, one that competes directly with established operators rather than merely supplying them with satellite coverage. The plan is technically ambitious, commercially uncertain and tied to Elon Musk's larger bet that robots and autonomous vehicles will create enormous demand for constant connectivity.

The starting point is substantial. Since opening for business in 2021, Starlink has attracted more than 12m individual subscribers, along with airlines and shipping companies. It also connects ordinary mobile phones beyond the reach of terrestrial masts through partnerships with operators such as T-Mobile. On October 8th, however, SpaceX confirmed that it intends to go much further and build a mobile network of its own.

## A network in orbit and on the ground

SpaceX's apparent strategy has three parts. First, it plans to expand its satellite fleet. American regulators have approved up to 15,000 satellites designed to send voice calls and data directly to smartphones. Satellites can cover vast areas, but each has limited capacity and spends only a short time over any one location. That makes them much better suited to remote regions than to dense cities.

The second part is therefore terrestrial. SpaceX wants to add small mobile masts, known as femtocells, to the receiver dishes used by Starlink broadband customers. Each dish could then provide short-range mobile coverage. The company also plans to build larger, conventional masts where more capacity is needed.

The third requirement is radio spectrum. In 2025 SpaceX spent \\$19.6bn on bandwidth from EchoStar for use by its phone-connected satellites. A separate deal announced by Grain Management reportedly valued another slice of low-frequency spectrum at \\$8bn. Those lower frequencies matter because they penetrate buildings more effectively, an essential capability for a network that hopes to serve people indoors as well as in the wilderness.

Together, the three layers would give SpaceX something broader than a satellite service: orbital coverage for hard-to-reach places, ground infrastructure for concentrated demand and spectrum rights that let ordinary phones connect to both.

## Today's customers may not justify the gamble

The commercial case is less clear. Satellite coverage sounds valuable to anyone imagining an emergency in the Alaskan wilderness, but SpaceX's partnership with T-Mobile offers little evidence that customers will pay much for it. T-Mobile's boss said in May that few customers had bought the satellite option and that it represented just 0.0002% of the network's total usage.

The incumbent operators are also responding. AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon have formed a joint venture that can pool their spectrum and purchase satellite capacity from several providers. That arrangement reduces their dependence on Starlink and gives them a way to offer satellite coverage without surrendering their customer relationships to SpaceX.

SpaceX's best argument is therefore not the mobile market as it exists now, but the one it expects to emerge. Gwynne Shotwell, its chief operating officer, predicts that autonomous vehicles and humanoid robots will sharply increase demand for connectivity. Tesla's machines could run over SpaceX's network, giving both companies an advantage. In this vision, the mobile network is infrastructure for a robotic economy rather than simply a better phone service.

## Starlink must finance a growing list of ambitions

That long-term logic does not remove the immediate financial risk. SpaceX is already pursuing an unusually broad collection of expensive projects. The article lists work on the Grok artificial-intelligence model, the Starship rocket, a large Louisiana launch complex, data centres in space and a politically sensitive lunar-lander contract with NASA.

The company raised \\$86bn in a public listing in June, then issued \\$25bn in debt only weeks later and plans to borrow more. Starlink is SpaceX's profit engine, helping absorb losses elsewhere in the business. Expanding it into terrestrial mobile infrastructure could create a powerful new market, but it also asks the division producing cash to fund another uncertain undertaking.

That tension is the article's central point. SpaceX has the satellites, launch capacity, customers and willingness to spend that make its plan credible. Yet the current appetite for satellite phone service is tiny, established operators are building their own defence and the hoped-for surge in robotic demand has not arrived. Starlink's mobile expansion may eventually look like farsighted infrastructure. For now, it is a costly wager on a future that SpaceX itself is trying to create.
