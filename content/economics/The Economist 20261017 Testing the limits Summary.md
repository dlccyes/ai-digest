Venture capitalists have poured money into a new class of artificial-intelligence startup built around star researchers and ambitious promises. These "neolabs" are supposed to move faster than established model-makers and discover approaches that larger organisations might miss. So far, however, their funding has advanced much faster than their products.

Twenty-nine neolabs have been founded since Safe Superintelligence launched in June 2024. Together they have raised \\$23bn. Yet fewer than 35% have released a commercial offering, and only a little over half have published code that developers can use. Much of that code is still presented as a research demonstration rather than a finished business.

The imbalance does not prove that the investment is wasted. OpenAI and Anthropic also needed years to turn research into products. It does show that investors have not yet established the predefined milestones that help them judge progress in other research-heavy industries.

## Valuations are running ahead of evidence

Safe Superintelligence is the clearest example. The company was last valued at \\$32bn and has raised \\$8bn, including a further \\$5bn from Nvidia after the chipmaker was shown undisclosed research. Its founder, Ilya Sutskever, says it will not release a product until it reaches superintelligence. In July he said the lab finally had work "worthy of scaling up", but offered no public details.

Thinking Machines Lab has produced more. After raising \\$2bn at a \\$12bn valuation, it released Tinker, a service for customising open-weight models developed elsewhere, followed by its own model, Inkling. The company has reportedly passed \\$200m in annualised revenue, a substantial result for a young startup. Even so, that figure is small beside the roughly \\$40bn valuation it is said to be seeking.

Popularity is similarly concentrated. Among the 20 most-used models on OpenRouter, the only neolab product is Jev, an inexpensive model that routes requests to other models rather than generating text itself. Its developer, Typesafe, raised just \\$40m before releasing it, then attracted another \\$870m once it had shown what the model could do. That sequence stands out in a field where enormous rounds often precede public evidence.

## Capital also buys scarce computing power

Neolabs face more than a product problem. New ideas can be copied quickly, as the competitors that appeared soon after Jev demonstrate. Startups must also compete with established labs for the computing capacity needed to train and test models.

That dependence gives chipmakers unusual influence over the field. Nvidia can offer access to scarce processors and has made 32 investments in startup model-makers, more than any other company or venture-capital firm in Radical Ventures' count. Reflection AI has already raised \\$800m from Nvidia and is reportedly discussing another large investment or a sale. AMD has joined the contest, paying \\$8bn for World Labs in September.

These relationships can help promising researchers obtain the infrastructure they need. They also make chipmakers important financiers of the startups competing to build the next generation of models.

## Research needs milestones as well as patience

Venture capital routinely accepts long odds in fields such as biotechnology. The difference is that biotechnology has familiar milestones: a successful phase of human trials gives investors evidence that justifies the next round. Neolabs are too new to have developed an equivalent process, and investors have not yet forced one upon them.

Some of these companies may eventually create breakthroughs worth far more than their current funding. Many will fail, as frontier-research startups normally do. The immediate weakness is not the risk itself, but the absence of frequent, comparable signals of progress.

The article's conclusion is therefore a demand for discipline rather than impatience. Investors should give difficult research time, but startups seeking ever larger sums must produce more regular results. Without milestones, early capital is being allocated largely on researcher reputation and confidence in broad ideas. That may be enough to start a laboratory. It is not enough to show that the laboratory is working.
