Donald Trump's pressure campaign against Canada was supposed to expose how little leverage a middle power has against a superpower. Instead, it has helped turn Mark Carney into a popular prime minister and given Canada a reason to tackle weaknesses it had tolerated for years. The Economist argues that other middle powers should study the result: they cannot replace the great powers or rebuild the old international order, but they can reduce their vulnerability by forming practical coalitions abroad and reforming themselves at home.

## Realism without surrender

Canada begins from an uncomfortable position. It has a middling economy, weak armed forces and deep dependence on the United States. Trump has exploited that imbalance with tariffs and threats, while describing Canada as an ungrateful beneficiary of American protection. The strain arrives as Alberta prepares for a referendum that could begin a path towards independence, taking with it vast oil reserves, although polls suggest that voters will choose to stay.

Yet Carney has refused to cave. His response starts with a realistic view of what countries below superpower rank can achieve. Middle powers will not replace America or China as the anchors of permanent institutions, and even the European Union often struggles to coordinate its members. But smaller groups can still act when they have a specific aim and shared interests.

Canada has pursued new trade deals and symbolic agreements, including talk of associate membership in the European Union. Japan offered an earlier model when it revived the Trans-Pacific Partnership after America withdrew. In defence, Canada is considering Swedish Gripen fighters as it reduces its reliance on American equipment; Norway is buying British frigates, and the United Arab Emirates is purchasing Ukrainian drones. Europe and Canada have also increased their support for Ukraine as America has pulled back.

These arrangements do not erase the underlying power imbalance. Europe's and Canada's support for Ukraine has not prevented Washington from abruptly changing course, as Trump's diesel diplomacy showed. Their strength lies instead in focused projects: keeping trade agreements alive, diversifying defence supply, supporting a threatened partner or resisting a discrete demand, as European countries did when Trump targeted Greenland.

## Strength begins at home

The more important Canadian lesson is domestic. Income per person fell from about 90% of America's level in the 1980s to 71% in 2022, much of the recent slide following a burst of low-skilled immigration. Internal trade barriers remained, dependence on the American market limited Canada's options and defence spending stood at only 1.3% of GDP.

Carney is using the external threat to push through changes that once looked politically impossible. He scrapped an unpopular consumer carbon tax, cancelled a large increase in capital-gains tax, cut the lowest income-tax rate and promised to restrain federal spending. His government has begun dismantling barriers to trade between provinces, plans a pipeline carrying Alberta's oil to the west coast and intends to lift defence spending to 2% of GDP.

Canada has also retaliated selectively against American tariffs, targeting imports from politically important states ahead of the midterm elections, while looking for ways to reduce its long-term dependence on its neighbour. The goal is greater freedom to manoeuvre if relations remain hostile even after Trump leaves office.

Reform has not yet produced the political punishment normally associated with difficult economic change. Carney was barely recognisable to Canadians two years ago; his approval rating is now approaching 70%. Trump's bullying has created a rally-round-the-flag effect, while Carney has tied national resilience to policies that might otherwise have appeared technical or painful.

## A model with limits

Canada's experience cannot simply be copied. It benefits from immense energy and mineral reserves and a safe geography, far from the most dangerous geopolitical flashpoints. Its liberalising reforms also need to go further and faster, while its search for alternatives must keep the country's options open if relations with America do not improve.

The broader principle travels well. Middle powers can cooperate on one-off projects with tangible aims and use external pressure to break domestic political deadlocks. Trump's aggression has made the world more dangerous, but Canada is using that threat to strengthen itself and preserve its options.
