Elon Musk has often argued that profit-making ventures such as Tesla and SpaceX do more good than conventional charity. Yet he also controls one of America's largest private foundations. With \\$14.7bn in assets at the end of 2024, the Musk Foundation was the country's fifth-largest charitable private foundation, slightly smaller than the Ford Foundation and more than twice the size of the Rockefeller Foundation.

The contrast is striking because the foundation says almost nothing about what it does. Its website consists of 33 words, its leadership team has three members and its tax filings provide the main public record of its activity. The Economist's review of filings from July 2020 through 2024 depicts a foundation that has been highly useful for sheltering appreciated Tesla shares from tax, slow to distribute its wealth and unusually inclined to direct grants towards organisations connected to Musk himself.

## A tax-efficient home for Tesla shares

Musk donated about \\$8bn in Tesla stock to the foundation in 2021 and 2022, when he received billions of dollars in shares and faced a large tax bill. Donating appreciated stock can provide two benefits. The donor avoids capital-gains tax on the increase in value and may claim a charitable deduction based on the much higher market price.

The gap between cost and value was extreme in this case. At the end of 2024, the Tesla shares held by the foundation had a book value of roughly \\$5.7m and an estimated market value of about \\$14bn. The arrangement is legal and common among wealthy founders. Its public justification, however, depends on the donated assets eventually supporting charitable work.

The Musk Foundation has moved slowly on that second part. American private foundations generally must distribute around 5% of their investment assets each year, though they receive a one-year grace period to cover a shortfall. According to FoundationMark, the Musk Foundation relied on that grace period in each of the four years for which filings were available. By the end of 2024, it still needed to disburse about \\$400m to satisfy the requirement.

## Money can move without reaching a cause

Even the reported grants overstate how much money has reached operating charities. Nearly one-tenth of the roughly \\$1bn disbursed from 2020 through 2024 went to Fidelity Charitable, which administers donor-advised funds. These accounts can hold money intended for charity indefinitely and impose few reporting requirements. Moving assets from a private foundation into such a fund is permitted, but one accounting expert describes it as shifting money from one bucket to another rather than putting it to work.

Most of the remaining money stayed close to Musk's network. More than 58% went to an organisation simply called The Foundation. It is led by Jared Birchall, the head of Musk's family office and one of the Musk Foundation's three officers, and it shares the foundation's address. Its stated purpose is to build schools devoted to science, technology, engineering and mathematics.

The public evidence of that educational programme is thin. Musk previously financed a small California school called Ad Astra, whose fewer than 50 pupils included his children, but it closed around the covid-19 pandemic. A new Ad Astra school is planned near SpaceX's base in Texas. The scale of the grants is therefore much easier to see than the scale of the work they support.

Other donations also overlap with Musk's business interests. The foundation contributed \\$55m to a children's-hospital fundraiser tied to a privately financed SpaceX mission. It made several grants to non-profit groups in southern Texas after a SpaceX rocket exploded and scattered debris in the area. Once payments to The Foundation and Fidelity are excluded, the average grant in 2024 was under \\$1.4m and ranged across unrelated causes such as sea-turtle conservation and spinal medicine. The article finds no clear strategy connecting them.

## Minimal administration, minimal accountability

The foundation's small staff is not necessarily a flaw. Recipients say its process is simple: a phone call, a cheque and an annual report. Its three leaders devote a combined average of only 2.1 hours a week to the organisation, and it pays no salaries. That hands-off approach resembles the grant-making style of Mackenzie Scott, whose giving Musk has criticised.

The difference is scale and transparency. Scott is one of the biggest donors in history and hands out money without prescribing its use or requiring regular reports. Musk's foundation has accumulated an enormous asset pool, repeatedly used the grace period for its annual payout requirement and channelled most of its grants through vehicles or organisations that reveal little about the ultimate results.

Musk has recently spent more directly on politics, giving at least \\$275m to the Republican Party ahead of the 2026 midterm elections. The money already placed in his foundation cannot be taken back, and he has occasionally suggested that larger charitable gifts will come later. For now, though, the foundation functions more convincingly as a tax-efficient store of wealth than as a coherent philanthropic institution. Its size promises public benefit; its record through 2024 shows far less of it than the balance sheet implies.
