Donald Trump's agreement to buy Russian diesel is presented as an attempt to lower fuel prices before America's midterm elections. The Economist argues that its real effect is far more serious: it gives Vladimir Putin revenue, helps protect Russian refineries, constrains Ukraine's refinery campaign and weakens the deterrence on which Ukraine and Europe depend.

The bargain is meagre even on its own terms. Russia initially promised 2.2m barrels of diesel, roughly what America consumes in 13 hours, with more supposedly to follow. It is uncertain whether Russia can deliver the fuel or whether the shipment would materially lower American prices. Yet Washington has already declared an energy ceasefire, pressed Ukraine to stop striking Russian refineries and suggested that American intelligence and weapons sales could be withheld if Kyiv refuses.

## A lopsided energy truce

The ceasefire does not bind Russia in practice. Moscow continues to attack Ukrainian cities and energy infrastructure while seeking to make them uninhabitable during winter. Ukraine, by contrast, is being asked to suspend attacks on refineries, one of the few tools it has for imposing direct economic costs on Russia.

The pressure is not merely rhetorical. On October 13th a Pentagon-controlled part of the Starlink satellite system was degraded for fast-moving Ukrainian drones, stopping them from hitting difficult targets in Russian-occupied territory. The episode showed how much leverage America retains over Ukraine's battlefield operations.

Putin stands to benefit in several ways. Diesel exports would earn revenue, and the agreement eases the threat that America might punish countries such as India and China for continuing to buy illicit Russian oil. If Washington helps shield Russian refineries from Ukrainian attacks, it also protects an industry that Ukraine has spent scarce resources trying to damage.

The article treats Trump's stated peace-making rationale as unconvincing. Putin represses Russians, kills Ukrainian civilians and supports Iran in its war with America. Repeated American indulgence does not moderate that behaviour; it signals that aggression can win concessions. Putin may have offered to press Iran towards a temporary ceasefire before the American elections, but his leverage is limited and any pause would probably be brief.

## Ukraine must preserve what America still provides

Ukraine has good reason not to respond by simply defying Washington and intensifying refinery strikes. American intelligence and Starlink help it target Russian headquarters, supply lines and radar. Ukrainian aircraft also need American ammunition to intercept incoming drones and missiles. Losing those capabilities could cost more than the refinery campaign gains.

Kyiv and its allies should instead expose the asymmetry of the arrangement. They are right to demand that any ceasefire cover all energy infrastructure, including the Ukrainian plants Russia continues to attack. Even if the White House refuses, making the imbalance visible could strengthen appeals to the Senate and remind Trump that the loss of Ukraine would make him look weak rather than successful.

The timing also matters. Once the midterm elections have passed, Russian diesel may lose much of its political value to Trump. He may then abandon the scheme or become distracted by another issue. Until then, Ukraine's task is to preserve the American intelligence, Starlink access and ammunition it still receives while increasing the political cost of the deal.

## Other targets can still impose costs

Restraint on refineries does not require Ukraine to stop striking Russia. The Economist proposes increasing attacks on other infrastructure, including data centres and distribution hubs. Such operations could threaten Putin's rule, make Russians more aware of the war being fought in their name and demonstrate that Ukraine can still hit large fixed targets without American assistance, including refineries if it becomes desperate.

The central danger is that Putin appears to have concluded he cannot win outright on the battlefield and is instead trying to exhaust Ukraine through attacks on its cities and energy supply. By restricting Ukraine for the sake of a small and uncertain fuel benefit, Trump is helping that strategy. The diesel deal is therefore not just a poor trade. It risks turning American leverage over an ally into one of Russia's most effective weapons.
