The blockade brought more blackouts, halted buses and higher food prices, but not the uprising or regime change he wanted. For decades Cuba imported most of its fuel from Venezuela through the government. That arrangement collapsed after American forces captured Nicolás Maduro and Washington threatened sanctions on anyone supplying Cuba's regime. Facing paralysis, Havana allowed private firms to import fuel for the first time since the 1959 revolution, while the United States authorised exporters to sell to them. Those shipments are now increasing. They do not yet include the heavy fuel needed by power stations, but they have put cars back on the road and small generators back to work.

A larger private sector could eventually weaken Gaesa, the military conglomerate that dominates much of the economy. Yet Washington is working against its own opportunity: in September the Trump administration reversed a rule that had allowed Cuban entrepreneurs to use American banks, forcing their accounts to be frozen.
