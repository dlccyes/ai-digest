Gibraltar's newly open border with Spain shows how economic necessity can soften even a centuries-old sovereignty dispute. On July 14th, border controls ended under a treaty between Britain and the European Union, bringing the British territory into much of Europe's system for frictionless movement and trade. The agreement does not settle who should own the Rock. Instead, it makes that argument less costly for the people who live and work around it.

The bargain is a practical repair to Brexit. Gibraltar has only about 40,000 residents, while more than 10,000 workers cross in each day from the neighboring Spanish town of La Linea. Many Gibraltarians own homes in Spain, and 96% voted to remain in the EU. A hard border would therefore have damaged both sides: Gibraltar depends on Spanish labor and access, while the surrounding region depends on Gibraltar's jobs and spending.

## Openness comes with alignment

The more than 1,000-page treaty took over five years to negotiate. It makes Gibraltar a de facto member of the Schengen passport-free area, though the territory calls the arrangement "fluidity" rather than freedom of movement. Citizens of Schengen countries do not automatically gain a right to live there, and Spanish police will check the passports of non-Gibraltarians arriving at the airport, whose flights currently come only from Britain.

Economic integration goes further. Gibraltar is joining the EU customs union for goods and accepting European product and tax rules. Its former tax-haven model will be narrowed by a 15% transaction tax on goods, rising to 17% by 2028, and by excise duties brought close to Spain's. The trade-off is clear: Gibraltar is giving up some regulatory autonomy in exchange for easier access to workers, customers and markets.

That access could support growth on both sides of the border. La Linea and the wider area around the port of Algeciras are poorer than much of Spain. Local leaders hope the treaty's stability will attract investment, while Gibraltar's airport may gain routes from elsewhere in Europe. Weak transport links, including a railway closed after landslides, will still constrain the region, but removing the border eliminates one of its largest self-imposed obstacles.

## Cooperation without surrender

The treaty succeeds by setting sovereignty aside. Spain has contested British control of Gibraltar since Britain acquired it in 1713, and some Spanish conservatives argue that Madrid should have demanded joint sovereignty. Yet past coercion, including General Franco's closure of the border in 1969, strengthened Gibraltar's separate "Mediterranean British" identity rather than weakening it.

The new arrangement reverses that logic. It lets Britain, Spain and Gibraltar protect their formal positions while cooperating on commerce, travel and potentially defense near a strategically important coast. A future Spanish government may change the rhetoric, but the mutual gains make the settlement harder to unwind.

The broader lesson is that integration need not require agreement on every constitutional question. By lowering the everyday price of political disagreement, the treaty could become a small model for a more pragmatic relationship between Britain and Europe after Brexit. The wall fell because prosperity mattered more than forcing a final answer about ownership.
