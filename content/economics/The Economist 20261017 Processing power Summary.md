For more than four decades, the semiconductor industry has followed a familiar cycle: surging demand encourages too much capacity, inventories build, prices collapse and the boom becomes a bust. Artificial intelligence has now produced a surge larger than most. Chip sales in the first half of 2026 grew at their fastest annual pace since the 1980s, while leading manufacturers reported repeated record profits.

Investors are beginning to ask whether the old cycle is about to reassert itself. The article's answer is nuanced. An AI slowdown would still hurt chipmakers, especially through lower prices, profits and share valuations. But decades of consolidation have left the most advanced manufacturing in the hands of a few powerful firms. They can expand more carefully, finance investment from cash and require customers to make long commitments. The next bust may therefore spread more of its pain to the technology companies buying the chips.

## A boom concentrated in AI

Almost all expected industry growth now depends on processors and memory for training and running AI models. That concentration explains both the extraordinary results and the growing anxiety. TSMC, the world's biggest contract chipmaker, has reported six consecutive quarters of record profits; Samsung has reported four. Yet an index of major semiconductor firms fell by more than 10% after October 6th even as companies issued strong results and forecasts.

Chipmakers and their customers are treating AI as a durable "supercycle". If that bet sours and AI-chip demand weakens, the question is how much of the resulting bust manufacturers themselves would bear.

That risk is real, but the industry's structure has changed. In the early 2000s, about 25 companies could manufacture the most advanced processors. Today only four can, and TSMC produces nearly all high-end chips. Advanced memory has followed the same path: more than 20 producers in 1990 have become three dominant suppliers, Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron. Chinese entrants remain behind the frontier because they cannot obtain the most advanced manufacturing equipment.

## Concentration encourages restraint

Fewer suppliers have more control over how quickly capacity expands and what customers pay. TSMC is expected to spend roughly \\$62bn on capital investment this year and \\$76bn next year. Those sums are enormous, but they are not growing as quickly as its revenue. Capital spending is expected to fall from 44% of revenue in 2023 to 36% this year.

The balance sheets are also stronger than in a debt-fuelled expansion. TSMC's new capacity will consume a little more than half of its operating cash flow this year, while Samsung's investment will use barely one-fifth. Both have modest debts relative to their cash piles. That reduces the pressure to keep factories running at any price if demand weakens.

The same concentration supports pricing discipline. DRAM memory costs about six times as much as it did in early 2023. TSMC has not reduced the price of the three-nanometre process it introduced in late 2022, even though manufacturing processes normally become cheaper after a few years. Building fabs in America, where construction takes roughly twice as long, further slows the arrival of new supply.

None of this makes overinvestment impossible. It does mean that today's leading manufacturers have both the incentive and the financial capacity to avoid flooding the market.

## Customers are underwriting the expansion

The largest change is contractual. Memory chips were once sold mainly through spot markets or short agreements. When demand weakened, customers could stop buying and suppliers absorbed the resulting collapse in prices. Scarce capacity and fewer alternatives now let manufacturers demand commitments years in advance.

Nvidia has committed more than \\$250bn to securing components and supply through January 2029. Micron says take-or-pay agreements have locked in 35% of its revenue through 2030, representing \\$32bn in commitments. Under these contracts, buyers must purchase the promised volumes or pay anyway. Most of those commitments involve cash deposits, which help fund new factories.

These arrangements do not eliminate cyclical risk; they redistribute it. If AI spending stalls, buyers will try to renegotiate contracts signed near the top of the market, but prepayments and take-or-pay terms leave them bearing at least part of the bust.

## A different kind of bust

An end to the AI boom would still send semiconductor prices and profits lower. Chip shares priced for years of rapid growth could fall sharply. Even dominant manufacturers would suffer as customers delayed orders and contested earlier agreements.

The article's central point is not that the chip cycle has disappeared. Consolidation, cash-funded investment and longer customer commitments have changed who bears its pain. A future downturn would still hurt chipmakers, but buyers would share more of the loss through prepayments and take-or-pay contracts.
