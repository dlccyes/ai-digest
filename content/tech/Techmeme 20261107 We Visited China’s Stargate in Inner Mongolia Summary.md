[Techmeme surfaced](https://www.techmeme.com/261108/p7#a261108p7) SemiAnalysis’s November 7 field report, [“We Visited China’s Stargate in Inner Mongolia.”](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/we-visited-chinas-stargate-in-inner) The report’s most important finding is not simply that China is building another enormous AI campus. It is that Envision Group is combining power generation, storage, electrical equipment, construction, and data-center operation in one project—and has put live reference implementations, including a 5 MW 800-volt pilot, on site.

## An 800-megawatt campus built around its power supply

Galaxy, in Ulanqab, is planned for as much as 800 MW of IT capacity, roughly comparable to the core of OpenAI’s Stargate campus in Abilene, Texas. Envision delivered Galaxy’s first 120 MW phase in under a year, and SemiAnalysis estimates that it cost about one-fifth as much as a comparable US build. The remaining phases, built to order for two unnamed Chinese internet companies, are expected by the end of 2027.

Galaxy uses China’s source-grid-load-storage model, linking its own wind farms, battery storage, dedicated line, and data-center load. Envision began as a renewable-energy systems company. For Galaxy’s first two phases, it built two wind farms with 420 MW of combined capacity, a dedicated transmission line, four-hour batteries, and the control systems that coordinate them with a 240 MW data-center load. The wind sites are about 80 kilometers apart, reducing the chance that both are calm at once, and the generation fleet is deliberately oversized. Envision aims for this system to supply more than 60 percent of the first two phases’ electricity from its own wind farms; the claim is substantial, but it is not the same as round-the-clock wind power.

This vertical integration extends inside the campus. Envision designed the low-voltage power modules, integrated the battery systems, helped design a prefabricated substation, and operates the site. Factory testing can move some commissioning work off the construction schedule, while the substation’s modular design reportedly cuts installation from about six months to less than three. The blade factory across the road is a physical expression of the same strategy: the data center creates demand for equipment and power that Envision already knows how to produce.

## A live test of the next power architecture

The most transferable engineering result is a 5 MW hall running on 800-volt direct current. Solid-state transformers take 10-kilovolt alternating current from the grid and convert it directly to 800-volt DC for the racks. That removes the separate low-voltage transformers, uninterruptible power supplies, and rectifiers found in conventional designs. SemiAnalysis says Nvidia’s roughly 600 kW Kyber racks cannot be served efficiently at today’s voltages, making a higher-voltage architecture necessary for the next generation of dense AI systems.

This is still a pilot, not proof that an entire 800 MW campus can use the architecture. Yet it is running at a live hyperscale site. SemiAnalysis estimates that about a quarter of Chinese data centers built over the past decade already use DC power, while roughly 97 percent of US sites still rely on AC UPS. China’s earlier adoption of 240-volt DC gave Galaxy a path toward 800 volts that US builders are only beginning to map.

Cooling follows a similarly pragmatic design. Liquid-cooled and air-cooled servers share one water loop, and the campus has no central chiller plant. SemiAnalysis says this design has operated at scale in northern China for years, while most US data centers retain chillers for air-cooled equipment and the flexibility to switch between cooling modes.

## Cost advantage, with important caveats

Galaxy sits inside a much larger regional buildout. SemiAnalysis says Inner Mongolia has 25 GW of announced data-center projects; a local report counted nearly 30,000 construction workers across 22 projects in the surrounding development zone. In China’s competitive market, monthly data-center rents have roughly halved in five years to about \\$40 per kilowatt before electricity, construction cost has fallen to less than \\$3 million per megawatt, and Inner Mongolian power costs about \\$0.055 per kilowatt-hour all-in.

Those figures should not be mistaken for a clean, audited comparison with the United States. SemiAnalysis sells a China data-center dataset, Envision supplied some of the project information, the two customers remain unnamed, and the public portion of the report does not break down the one-fifth cost claim line by line. Labor, land, permitting, reliability requirements, financing, equipment, and what each estimate includes can all differ. The report establishes an operating reference point, not a universal cost law.

SemiAnalysis reports that Inner Mongolia faces no comparable anti-data-center backlash, but it provides no public evidence for that observation in this article, so it should be treated as the publication’s characterization. SemiAnalysis says Envision aims to supply more than 60 percent of the first two phases’ power from its own wind farms; it does not quantify the remaining share or identify its source. The report leaves the full water and grid effects for later analysis.

The durable lesson is narrower and more useful than “China builds faster.” AI infrastructure is becoming an energy-system product. Galaxy suggests that vertical integration can give a developer more control over cost and schedule, though SemiAnalysis says it is too early to know whether that becomes a durable advantage. Its 800-volt pilot and chillerless cooling loop are designs other builders can study. Its policy environment and cost structure cannot simply be copied—and should not be treated as if they can.
