The most important idea in multi-agent engineering is not adding more agents. It is arranging work so that plausible mistakes are cheap to reject. Google Research’s official blog develops that idea in [“Advancing science & engineering with Antigravity’s /teamwork”](https://research.google/blog/advancing-science-engineering-with-antigravitys-teamwork-feature/), a wide-ranging account of how specialized Gemini agents were paired with formal proof checkers, unmodified test suites, simulators, benchmarks, and hardware counters.

The post reports results across mathematics, chemistry, distributed systems, code migration, kernel optimization, and Google’s production infrastructure. The individual achievements are striking, but the reusable engineering lesson sits underneath them: long-running agent systems become more trustworthy when exploration, execution, criticism, and verification are separate roles connected by objective gates.

## Partition the context, then verify the result

Google identifies two failure modes that make a single long-running model unreliable. Generation is stochastic, so one attempt may miss a valid solution even when another would find it. Long contexts also degrade over multi-day projects as evidence, intermediate decisions, and failed approaches accumulate.

`/teamwork` addresses the first problem through parallel search and dedicated falsifiers. Several agents can explore candidate solutions, but acceptance is delegated to a mechanism appropriate to the domain: Lean 4 for formal proofs, existing tests for software, a spectral simulator for molecular structures, or timing and hardware counters for kernels. The agents can propose and revise; the verifier decides whether a result survives.

It addresses the second problem by partitioning context. Each specialist receives only the files and tools needed for its stage. Coordinators pass work to fresh successors, while critics and auditors examine outputs from clean contexts rather than inheriting the producer’s entire reasoning history. The workflows are expressed as reusable natural-language patterns, and the system routes tasks to patterns such as distributed search, sequential solve-and-revise, long proof, or document review.

This design makes the harness, rather than the base model alone, the unit of engineering. A capable model supplies search and synthesis, but the harness controls context boundaries, chooses when to branch, and decides which evidence is strong enough to advance.

## Proof work shows why the gates matter

Formal mathematics provides the clearest version of this architecture because Lean’s kernel checks every proof term. Human experts still decide whether the formal theorem states the intended claim, but once that boundary is fixed, the proof cannot compile by rhetoric or by changing the test.

For the Courtade–Kumar conjecture in information theory, researchers reduced the problem to a 14-variable optimization and supplied an analytical lower bound. Gemini agents then partitioned the parameter space, developed further bounds and interval-arithmetic certificates, extended the result beyond the balanced case, and helped produce a 50-million-line Lean formalization.

The same pattern scaled to verification projects that would overwhelm ordinary review. A `/teamwork` variant called LEAP generated a roughly 1.8-million-line formalization of the Weil conjectures through 28,800 subagent invocations. It also formalized recent results involving Hilbert’s tenth problem over rings of integers and finite-time blowup for forced three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations. These are claims reported by Google and their scholarly validation ultimately belongs to the research community, but machine checking changes the evidence standard: the enormous derivations can be replayed rather than accepted as unexamined model output.

Google applied a related review pattern to 578 submissions for STOC 2027. In an ongoing survey of 70 participating authors, 90% rated the feedback mostly or almost entirely factual, while more than 30% said it found substantive proof bugs or mathematically significant typos. The numbers are self-reported and the survey is still small, yet they show how an agentic system can support reviewers without pretending to replace the final human judgment about a paper’s contribution.

## Objective evaluators turn coding into search

The same architecture becomes an optimization loop in software and systems work. The agents generate changes; builds, compatibility tests, profilers, and performance measurements determine which changes remain candidates.

For code migration, Google scheduled modules only after their dependencies compiled and tested each leaf against the original unmodified suite. A two-week campaign completed the remaining C++-to-Rust port of the Zircon microkernel, adding about 800,000 lines of safe Rust that passed the kernel tests and booted on Arm64 hardware. Another campaign rewrote Google’s `libgav1` AV1 decoder into roughly 170,000 lines of Rust with bit-exact conformance. The first version was about four times slower than the C++ implementation; after agents received profiling tools and a performance objective, the Rust version reportedly became 20–30% faster on both x86-64 and Arm.

Kernel tuning required a different verifier design. Full JAX and Pallas TPU evaluations took 7 to 32 minutes, too slow for broad search. Four agents built 23-second proxy evaluators, or “yardsticks,” and continuously checked their rankings against periodic full hardware runs. This reduced the cost of rejecting weak candidates while preserving contact with the real metric. Google reports geometric-mean speedups of 4.09× across 17 JAXBench kernels and 2.99× across seven hand-optimized Pallas kernels used for vLLM TPU inference.

The production examples are narrower but especially useful. Google says reusable `/teamwork` workflows identified more than one pebibyte of fleet-wide memory-saving opportunities. A Borg scheduling workflow shipped two changes that reduced TPU workload preemption by 24% on some hardware types while maintaining workload service-level objectives and cluster utilization. In both cases, the system’s value came from searching a large configuration space while measured operational constraints remained in charge.

## The broader engineering takeaway

Multi-agent systems are often described as organizational metaphors: a manager delegates to workers, who report back. Google’s results suggest a more precise model. The useful abstraction is a search process wrapped around trusted evaluators, with context deliberately reset at role boundaries. Parallelism helps when candidate work can be separated; sequential revision is better when modules are tightly coupled; and neither is safe without a gate the agents cannot casually redefine.

That also explains the limits. A verifier proves only what its specification measures. Lean cannot decide whether humans formalized the right theorem, a test suite can preserve an old bug, a proxy benchmark can drift from real hardware, and production experiments can optimize a local metric at the expense of an omitted one. Human experts therefore remain responsible for defining the objective, inspecting the evidence, and choosing what ships.

The durable lesson is that model capability and system reliability are different engineering problems. Stronger models widen the search space. Reliable harnesses constrain that search with clean contexts, independent criticism, unmodified tests, and measurements tied to the real environment. The impressive outputs in Google’s post matter, but the architecture that makes them inspectable is the part most teams can reuse.
