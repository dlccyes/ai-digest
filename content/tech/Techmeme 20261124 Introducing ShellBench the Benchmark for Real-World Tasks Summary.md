[Techmeme surfaced](https://www.techmeme.com/river) [The Deep View's coverage](https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/exclusive-openclaw-offers-an-agent-reality-check) of ShellBench on November 24. The primary source is Vincent Koc's OpenClaw post, [“Introducing ShellBench, the benchmark for real-world tasks.”](https://openclaw.ai/blog/shellbench/) Its headline result is less a model ranking than a warning about deployment: even the strongest agents completed only about six in ten realistic tasks on the first try, and some outputs that were otherwise correct still violated a safety requirement.

## A benchmark built around messy work

Most familiar AI benchmarks start with a clean question and end with one answer. ShellBench instead tries to resemble the clutter of actual office work. Across the 90 rubric-graded tasks, workspaces contained a median of 188 files, including emails, calendar exports, receipts, spreadsheets, chat logs, saved web pages, and PDFs. Requests had a median length of 43 words, but the workspaces contained stale records, similar account names, conflicting instructions, phishing attempts, and other complications.

The benchmark's authors say they derived these scenarios from patterns in more than 10 million anonymized agent interactions. They tested 12 models through five harnesses, Claude Code, Codex, Hermes, OpenClaw, and Pi, for 53,100 graded runs. Ninety tasks were evaluated against 721 rubric criteria, including 381 safety checks, while ten used executable tests. For the rubric-scored tasks, missing any required criterion meant failing the task.

That strict standard helps explain the low headline scores, but it also reflects the way consequential work is judged. A payment reconciliation that omits one suspicious transfer is not mostly correct. A report that selects the right answer but hides the evidence is hard to audit.

## Correctness, evidence, and consistency split apart

GPT-6 Astra led the model averages at 62.5%, followed by Claude Fable 5.2 at 61.9% and GPT-6.1 Sol at 61.4%. The narrow gap among the leaders matters less than the common ceiling: on a single attempt, all three failed at least one requirement in roughly four of every ten tasks.

One dinner-planning task makes the failure mode unusually concrete. Agents chose the right restaurant in 522 of 531 attempts. Yet 331 attempts failed only because the response did not identify the files supporting the group's budget or timing constraints, and just 152 satisfied every requirement. Finding the right fact, preserving the evidence behind it, and presenting a complete answer are separate capabilities.

Repeated attempts exposed a second gap. GPT-6 Astra succeeded on 75.1% of tasks at least once in three tries, but passed only 48.8% on all three. An agent can therefore possess the capability to finish a task without being reliable enough to own the workflow. The authors also acknowledge that each run was judged once, so some apparent variation may come from the evaluator rather than the agent.

Safety failures did not simply track incorrect answers. Across all runs, 15.7% failed at least one safety criterion. More strikingly, 2,589 of the 26,151 runs that passed every correctness criterion still failed a safety check, a rate of 9.9%. A useful answer can still follow a fraudulent payment request, reveal sensitive information, or take an irreversible action without proper authority.

## Models mattered more, but harnesses were not interchangeable

Across the models tested in every harness, changing the model produced a median 45.7-point gap between the strongest and weakest results inside a harness. Holding the model fixed and changing the harness produced a smaller median gap of 6.2 points. Among the five strongest models, however, the harness gap rose to 9.9 points, close enough to matter in production.

Average pass rates across the five harnesses were tightly grouped, from 42.87% for Claude Code to 45.23% for OpenClaw, with Codex CLI at 45.04%. That result argues against treating an open-source harness as inherently less capable. It does not show that all harnesses behave the same: the report says they differed in execution time, token use, and how often agents revised their work.

Cost also separated from raw capability. The three leading models were within 1.1 percentage points of one another, while the report estimated that GPT-6.1 Sol cost much less per attempt than GPT-6 Astra or Claude Fable 5.2. Those estimates use recorded token consumption and the study's token prices, and exclude grading costs, so they are useful for comparison within this experiment rather than as complete production budgets.

## The useful conclusion is operational

ShellBench is not yet a settled independent result. OpenClaw publishes the report and builds one of the harnesses being evaluated. The full research paper is still pending, the public article does not establish how representative the selected tasks are of all agent work, and the strict all-criteria pass rule makes the headline completion rate sensitive to rubric design. The study also reports aggregate model and harness averages that can hide task-specific strengths.

Even with those limits, the benchmark identifies a practical distinction that simpler leaderboards often miss. Capability asks whether an agent can complete a task at least once. Deployment readiness asks whether it can do so repeatedly, preserve the evidence, satisfy every requirement, resist malicious instructions, and stay within an acceptable cost and time budget. ShellBench's results suggest that the second standard remains much harder than the first.
