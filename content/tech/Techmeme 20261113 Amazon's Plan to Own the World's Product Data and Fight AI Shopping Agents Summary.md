[Techmeme surfaced](https://www.techmeme.com/261113/p37) Greg Bensinger's November 13 Reuters report, [“Amazon's plan to own the world's product data and fight AI shopping agents.”](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/amazons-plan-own-worlds-product-data-fight-ai-shopping-agents-2026-11-13/) The article describes an internal proposal called World Product Graph: a structured catalog of nearly every product sold online, including goods Amazon does not stock. The project is not a confirmed launch. It is a revealing statement of what Amazon believes an AI shopping system needs in order to become a trusted intermediary rather than a chatbot layered over an incomplete store catalog.

## The shopping contest is becoming a data contest

Amazon is already strong when shoppers know exactly what they want. A 2025 Morgan Stanley survey cited by Reuters found that 41 percent of those searches began on Amazon, compared with 23 percent on Google and 18 percent on Walmart. Amazon was much weaker at the earlier research stage: only 16 percent of shoppers started broad product research there, versus 57 percent on Google.

World Product Graph is meant to close that gap. According to the internal document, Amazon cannot become the destination for every shopping task while people still need Google, brand websites, review sites, and forums to understand how products relate to one another. A useful catalog must know more than a listing's title, price, and seller. It must distinguish the most recent model or colorway of a vacuum cleaner or tablet, map relationships among products, and support subjective comparisons such as which item best fits a particular customer's history and preferences.

The proposal calls for brands and retailers to contribute product data continuously while Amazon also discovers how brands organize products on their own sites. Reuters reports that the plan could include crawling rival retailers. The document says Coca-Cola and Bayer had agreed to contribute data, while Amazon held a senior-level meeting with Procter & Gamble about a trial. Reuters could not determine how Amazon would crawl other sites or when it would need permission.

This would extend Amazon's role beyond operating a marketplace. A sufficiently complete graph could become the reference layer that shopping agents consult even when the eventual product is sold elsewhere. Existing services such as Buy for Me and Shop Direct already let Amazon present goods from outside its own inventory and act as an intermediary in the purchase. World Product Graph would supply the underlying map: what exists, how products differ, and which relationships matter.

## Better data is also an answer to unreliable and expensive AI

The internal proposal is unusually direct about the limits of Amazon's current assistant. It says many answers from Rufus—renamed Alexa for Shopping in May—were factually wrong or incomplete, while customers often lacked the expertise to notice. It also says the system was handling about 83 million searches each week and was expensive to run.

Those claims explain why a product graph is more than a search-index expansion. Language models are good at expressing an answer, but they still need accurate, current facts. If a model must infer relationships from scattered pages on every request, it can confuse revisions, invent compatibility, or miss a newer product. A maintained graph can move much of that work into a structured data layer that is easier to validate and reuse. The proposal says a richer catalog could also let Amazon answer more queries with smaller, cheaper models.

Amazon disputes the memo's current relevance. It told Reuters that draft, outdated documents can present an incomplete picture and that a brainstorming document does not necessarily represent its plans. The company also said the usage figures were significantly outdated and that Alexa for Shopping was roughly 97 percent accurate globally. That response does not resolve the difference between the internal criticism and the public accuracy figure: Reuters does not provide the measurement methods, error definitions, or dates needed to compare them.

## Whoever controls the catalog can shape where agents shop

The strategic threat is not merely that another assistant recommends a rival retailer. Independent agents can separate product discovery from the store that owns the interface. A customer might ask Meta's Muse, Perplexity, Grok, or another service to compare products and complete a purchase without visiting Amazon directly. That weakens Amazon's control over the customer relationship and could divert both sales and advertising demand.

Amazon has already resisted that access. Reuters reports that Muse has attracted more than 10 million users in the United States and Canada since launching in September; Amazon blocked it from its retail site that same month. Amazon is also pursuing litigation intended to stop Perplexity from accessing the store in a similar way. World Product Graph represents the complementary strategy: instead of only excluding outside agents, build the most comprehensive product database so that those agents have reason to rely on Amazon.

That creates a new platform contest. In traditional e-commerce, a retailer's advantage comes from selection, price, logistics, and customer traffic. In agentic commerce, an additional advantage is the canonical product model that determines what an agent can find, compare, and explain. If Amazon owns that model, it can remain central even when the shopper never browses an Amazon results page. It can also protect its advertising business by keeping product discovery tied to data and transactions it controls.

The same power creates governance problems. A universal catalog would have to reconcile conflicting manufacturer and retailer claims, normalize identifiers across markets, correct stale records, and decide which attributes are authoritative. Crawling competitors raises permission and competitive questions. Recommendations based on Amazon's graph could favor products with richer supplied data or relationships that Amazon can monetize, even when the graph is technically accurate. The Reuters report describes the commercial ambition, not safeguards for ranking neutrality, provenance, appeals, or outside access.

## A plan, not a product

The evidence comes from an internal PR/FAQ, Amazon's narrative format for proposing new services. The document carried an aspirational October 1, 2027 release date, and Reuters reports that staff were being reassigned to the effort under Selection & Catalog Systems vice president Robert Tekiela. But not every PR/FAQ becomes a product; the project could change substantially or be abandoned, and Reuters could not establish whether the date was realistic.

That caveat is central. The report does not show that Amazon has already built a comprehensive global catalog, secured permission to crawl competitors, or solved product identity and data quality at internet scale. It does show that Amazon views catalog completeness as a bottleneck for reliable shopping agents and as a defense against losing the shopping interface to other AI companies.

The durable lesson is that the most valuable component of an AI shopping system may not be the conversational model. It may be the maintained body of facts underneath it: the identifiers, relationships, compatibility rules, versions, prices, and provenance that let an agent know what it is buying. World Product Graph remains a draft, but the competitive logic is already visible. The company that builds the best product map can influence which stores agents see, which comparisons they make, and where the transaction ends.
