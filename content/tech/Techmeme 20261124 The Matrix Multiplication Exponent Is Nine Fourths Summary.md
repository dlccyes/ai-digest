If the proof survives expert review, it closes a question that has been open since fast matrix multiplication began in 1969. The surprise is not another faster algorithm. OpenAI had already claimed in October that algorithms over the complex numbers can approach exponent `2.25`. The new result goes in the other direction, arguing that no formal arithmetic circuit can do asymptotically better.

## What the exponent measures

The schoolbook method multiplies two `n` by `n` matrices with work proportional to `n³`. Strassen showed that seven multiplications could replace the usual eight for two-by-two blocks, lowering the exponent to about `2.807` when the construction is applied recursively. Decades of work pushed the best upper bound below `2.372`, while the obvious lower bound remained `2` because the result itself contains `n²` entries.

That gap supported a famous possibility: perhaps matrix multiplication could be done in nearly quadratic time, with exponent `2` plus an arbitrarily small margin. OpenAI's October paper placed the complex-field exponent at no more than `2.25`. Subsequent work extended the bound to every field through expositions and alternative arguments, including a [constructive proof based on ordinary block recursion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28627) and a [tensor-value proof](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09716).

The November paper claims the matching lower bound. For every side length `n` of at least two, it says a formal arithmetic circuit needs at least `n^(9/4) / 2` nonscalar multiplication or division gates. For bilinear algorithms of the kind used by Strassen, the claimed multiplication count is at least `n^(9/4)`. Combined with the known upper bound, those results make the exponent exactly `9/4`.

The scope matters. This is a result about exact formal arithmetic: matrix entries are treated as independent variables, and a circuit must produce the matrix-product polynomials identically. The finite-size bound counts multiplication and division gates, not every addition, memory access, communication step, or machine instruction. It is nevertheless broad across number systems, covering real and complex numbers as well as finite fields.

## The proof turns faster multiplication into impossible compression

The paper argues by contradiction. Assume matrix multiplication had an exponent below `9/4`. That assumption would let the authors extract a bilinear identity, move the construction to a finite field, and compress repeated tensor networks more aggressively than should be possible.

The comparison problem is list duplicate detection. A network receives lists of bit strings and must determine whether every entry in each list is distinct. Sorting gives a compact network for the task, but the paper also derives a minimum network volume. Repeatedly applying the compression implied by faster matrix multiplication would eventually shrink the duplicate-detection network below that minimum, producing the contradiction.

Most of the 111-page manuscript develops the compression argument. A [companion paper on tensor geometry](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Euclidean-geometry-in-the-asymptotic-spectrum-of-tensors-November-24-2026/README.md) supplies the framework used to assign costs to information shared among sites in a tensor network. This is not merely an optimization of the techniques behind the October algorithm. It is a proposed new lower-bound mechanism, historically the much harder side of the problem.

It also proves that allowing division preserves the square exponent. The paper's explicit finite-`n` bound of `n^(9/4) / 2` nonscalar multiplication or division gates remains outside the formalization.

## A theoretical limit, not an instant speedup

An exact exponent would settle how the arithmetic count grows in the limit; it would not make today's matrix multiplication suddenly faster. Fast asymptotic constructions can carry enormous constants and overhead, while practical GPU workloads are constrained by memory movement, numerical formats, parallelism, and matrix sizes far below the regime where a theoretical exponent dominates. The new paper supplies a lower bound, not a production kernel.

Its importance is still difficult to overstate. For 57 years, researchers knew matrix multiplication was faster than cubic but could not rule out an exponent arbitrarily close to the quadratic output size. If `9/4` is correct, that optimistic endpoint is impossible in the formal arithmetic model, and a central problem in algorithms has an unexpectedly clean answer.
