[Techmeme surfaced](https://www.techmeme.com/261121/p10) Ars Technica's November 20 report, [“Researcher plants 2m canaries into the AI supply chain. A privacy disaster surfaced.”](https://arstechnica.com/security/2026/11/a-leaky-ai-supply-chain-is-exposing-secret-user-data-at-an-alarming-rate/) The report covers a five-month [Dragos investigation](https://www.dragos.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-security-canary-tokens) that placed unique, trackable secrets into nearly two million AI prompts. The experiment found three different failure modes: attackers inside inference providers, marketplace resellers reading the traffic they handled, and providers retaining or forwarding data despite privacy settings.

The larger lesson is architectural. An AI request may look like one API call, but it can cross a router, another router, a reseller, a model host, and the model developer. Every hop receives plaintext. A “Zero Data Retention” label is therefore only as strong as the weakest organization and the least visible subcontractor in the route.

## Turning prompts into traceable evidence

Researcher HD Moore gave every test prompt a unique URL, DNS name, and AWS credential tied to an empty, fully logged account. The broader set also included private repository references and passwords. If anyone later opened a URL, cloned a repository, tried a password, or used an AWS key, the event could be traced back to one prompt, provider, route, and privacy setting.

This design addresses a hard measurement problem. A privacy policy cannot prove what happened to a particular request, and ordinary sensitive text leaves no signal after it is copied. A canary does. Dragos also built a form of traceroute for inference. Image fetches exposed network addresses, while deliberately malformed requests accumulated provider-specific error wrappers. Those signals let the team identify hidden intermediaries that the advertised route did not disclose.

The experiment found this routing complexity repeatedly. AI gateways offer one endpoint across many models and providers, often selecting capacity by price, availability, or policy. Some upstream providers were themselves routers. Among roughly two thousand routes Dragos traced, several hundred contained a system it could not identify, while a smaller number contacted companies absent from the advertised path. The convenience of unified access had created a data supply chain that customers could not reliably inspect.

## Three failures with different causes

The most serious result was evidence that attackers were copying requests from three hosting providers. In the largest case, a few hundred planted AWS keys sent during short windows were stolen, including keys from requests rejected before a model ran. That location matters: it suggests collection at the provider's front door rather than leakage from model output. The stolen bait credentials generated tens of thousands of AWS API calls as attackers listed S3 buckets, searched Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, enabled Bedrock models, and attempted to create persistent IAM access.

Secrets sent to two providers were used almost immediately from the same rented server, which points to one adversary operating across both. Dragos says the providers did not know about the breaches until it notified them. Because the canaries were mixed into normal traffic, the researchers infer that real prompts passing through the same collection points were probably copied too. The canaries establish unauthorized access to the test data, however, not the contents or number of real customer prompts affected.

A second class of leakage required no provider compromise. Some low-price marketplace sellers could turn on request logging as part of normal operation. Dragos sent equivalent requests for the same model through different sellers and saw later credential use only when traffic passed through particular sellers. In one case, someone on a home connection opened both secret URLs routed through one seller and guessed usernames. In another, a person manually tried seeded passwords over SSH and later tested several AWS keys. Price competition had made prompt access itself a possible subsidy for cheap inference.

The third failure involved privacy promises rather than credential theft. Systems associated with one Chinese model lab fetched secret URLs from more than half the prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds marked for no collection or zero retention. The same fetch pattern appeared when U.S. hosts resold those models, showing that selecting a domestic endpoint did not necessarily keep data domestic. One secret link appeared in a company chat 24 days after a ZDR-marked request, evidence that a full copy of the prompt remained available well after inference.

## Provider responses make the result harder to dismiss

The investigation is not supported only by Dragos's interpretation. [Together AI disclosed](https://www.together.ai/blog/security-event-prompt-leakage-for-requests-in-june-and-july-of-2026) that an AI coding agent had committed a credential to a public repository. The credential exposed a private Hugging Face repository containing authorized prompt samples from several model endpoints. An unauthorized party downloaded those samples and tested credentials found inside them, including five Dragos canaries. Together says it has not found evidence of leakage beyond that incident, is still reviewing the affected data, and has changed its credential, pull-request, retention, and coding-agent controls.

[OpenRouter said](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/data-processing-incidents-on-inference-providers/) the research did not show a breach of OpenRouter itself, but did reveal problems at specific inference providers. It off-boarded three providers, removed 125 endpoints from ZDR eligibility, notified potentially affected customers, and began adapting the canary method for continuing checks. It also started verifying network paths to find requests forwarded to undisclosed third parties. This response turns the study's central idea into an operating control: privacy claims should be tested continuously, not accepted as static metadata.

## What the study proves, and what it does not

The evidence is unusually strong at showing that particular planted prompts escaped their expected boundaries. Unique secrets, controlled accounts, provider-specific comparisons, and subsequent disclosures make accidental coincidence implausible. The experiment also separates three mechanisms that are easy to blur together: an external breach, deliberate or opportunistic reseller logging, and a provider's own data handling.

It does not establish that every router or provider leaks prompts, that all ZDR requests are retained, or that a gateway is inherently less secure than a direct provider. The public report does not include the complete dataset, and some affected organizations remain unnamed while investigations continue. A triggered canary proves that someone or some system saw the planted secret, but reconstructing the responsible hop still depends on routing evidence and provider cooperation. Dragos also sells security products, so its recommendations should be weighed separately from the incident evidence.

The practical conclusion is narrower and more useful than “never use AI.” Sensitive workloads should minimize the number of organizations that can read them, pin upstream providers instead of accepting an opaque cheapest-route pool, filter credentials before prompts leave the application, and monitor the path with canaries. Self-hosting gives the strongest boundary but is costly; going directly to a first-tier provider still carries breach and policy risk, but reduces the number of contracts and hidden processors.

AI inference has inherited the classic supply-chain problem while making the payload more valuable. Prompts can contain source code, customer data, internal strategy, or credentials, and agents can assemble those materials automatically. The Dragos experiment shows that a privacy badge is not an end-to-end guarantee. The trustworthy unit is the observed route, including every intermediary, plus evidence that its promises survive contact with real traffic.
