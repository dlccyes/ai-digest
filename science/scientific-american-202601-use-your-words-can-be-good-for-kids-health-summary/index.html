    Naming Changes the Experience

    “Use your words” is often delivered as an emergency instruction after a child’s emotions have already overwhelmed the room. Psychologist and neuroscientist J. David Creswell argues that the phrase points to something real, but its power lies less in stopping a meltdown on command than in a skill built over time: putting an internal experience into language can change how the mind processes it.

    The article draws first on four decades of research into expressive disclosure, in which people write or speak about difficult experiences. Across this work, participants have reported benefits ranging from fewer medical visits and less pain to improved immune function and better outcomes for asthma and arthritis. Studies with adults also suggest that the structure matters. Writing about a stressful event three or four times on nearby days tends to work better than scattering the sessions, and sessions of at least 15 minutes appear more useful than very brief attempts. Speaking can work as well as writing; in one direct comparison it did better, possibly because people can express more material aloud in the same amount of time.

    These findings help explain why psychotherapy can be transformative. In one study, psychologist Jonathan M. Adler followed adults as they wrote about themselves over 12 therapy sessions. Improvements in their narratives came first: participants increasingly described themselves as people with choices and influence over their lives. Better mental health followed. The sequence does not by itself prove that a more agentic story caused the improvement, but it supports the idea that changing how an experience is organized in language can accompany recovery.

    From Emotion Labels to Altered Memories

    One proposed mechanism is “affect labeling,” the act of naming an emotion such as anger or fear. Neuroscience studies indicate that doing so recruits emotion-regulation circuits in the prefrontal cortex. In an exposure-therapy study, people with a spider phobia who labeled their feelings while sitting near a tarantula showed a smaller physiological stress response to spiders one week later than participants who used strategies such as distraction. Naming did not erase the fear; it appeared to help the brain handle it differently.

    Narration may also work through memory reconsolidation. Recalling a troubling event does not simply replay a fixed recording. Retrieval makes the memory temporarily open to revision before it is stored again. Describing the event can add context, meaning and a sense of resolution, potentially reducing the distress it later evokes. Words, on this account, can reshape both an immediate feeling and the longer autobiographical story in which that feeling lives.

    Evidence in children is more limited but points in the same direction. A recent review found small but meaningful improvements in emotional well-being from expressive-writing programs for children and adolescents ages 10 to 18, with some indication of academic benefits among young people who had significant emotional difficulties. Younger children may use storytelling or drawing with adult guidance instead of formal writing.

    The article is a research-informed parenting column, not a systematic review. It does not provide sample sizes, effect estimates or methodological detail for most of the studies it cites, and its broad health claims should not be read as a guarantee that expression will help every child or condition. Children with speech delays or neurodivergence may find words especially inaccessible under stress. Creswell recommends meeting them with alternatives such as pictures, physical prompts and calm co-regulation rather than treating speech as the only valid route.

    Practice before the Meltdown

    The least effective time to teach emotional language is often the moment when a child is already flooded. Strong emotion can temporarily impair the ability to think clearly, choose words or speak at all. Creswell therefore emphasizes conversations outside the crisis: a bedtime question about the hardest part of the day, a gentle invitation to name what happened, or a parent modeling the process by saying that they feel frustrated and need a breath.

    Repeated in ordinary moments, these exchanges build vocabulary and create a habit of reflection. They can also give children a sense of agency. A child who can identify anger, connect it to an event and imagine a different response begins to see emotion as something understandable and navigable rather than as a force that simply takes control.

    Language is not a switch that turns distress off. It is closer to a tool for organizing experience—one that works through practice, requires adaptation and sometimes needs the support of therapy or other care. The deeper lesson behind “use your words” is not that a child should perform calmness on demand. It is that, with patience and the right support, learning to narrate inner life can help make feelings and memories more manageable.
