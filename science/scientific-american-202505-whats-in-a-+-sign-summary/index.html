    Mathematics had to invent its language

    Symbols such as +, −, =, 0 and ∀ look timeless, as though they emerged fully formed with the ideas they represent. In fact, mathematical notation developed through centuries of regional rivalry, technological change and individual ambition. In an interview with Scientific American, mathematician Raúl Rojas argues that this human history is not decorative background. It explains how abstract concepts became easier to express, teach and extend.

    Before notation became standardized, European mathematicians often wrote the same ideas in incompatible ways. Equality was usually described in words. Robert Recorde introduced an elongated version of the familiar equal sign in 16th-century England, but René Descartes used a rotated symbol resembling Taurus, and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz preferred a wedge. The operators now written + and − competed with the letters p and m, which were favored in Italy.

    These contests were not settled by a central authority. Printing made it possible for many proposed symbols to circulate, and the choices associated with influential mathematicians gradually accumulated users. Recorde’s parallel lines, along with + and −, ultimately won what Rojas describes as popularity battles. Modern notation therefore reflects historical network effects as much as logical necessity: a symbol became standard because enough people adopted it, printed it and taught it.

    Zero shows how much a mark can carry

    Zero has an even longer and less settled history. The Babylonians used a positional base-60 number system but initially represented an empty place with a gap rather than a numeral. Rojas’s account follows positional notation through the Greek world to India, where a written symbol for zero developed. Researchers continue to debate the oldest surviving examples, and new archaeological finds periodically push the evidence further back.

    That history matters because 0 does more than label “nothing.” As a placeholder, it distinguishes numbers such as 12 and 102; as a number in its own right, it supports arithmetic and later algebra. The familiar oval compresses a long conceptual achievement into a mark that people use without noticing. Its uncertain origin also illustrates why the history of mathematics rarely belongs to a single inventor or culture.

    Notation can carry moral history as well as mathematical meaning. Rojas points to Gerhard Gentzen’s ∀, the “for all” symbol used in logic. Gentzen was a gifted mathematician who joined both the Nazi Party and the SS and accepted a post in occupied Prague that displaced others. He remained after the war, was imprisoned and died of starvation. The symbol survived and became ordinary, while its creator’s career remained entangled with collaboration and self-justification. A clean abstraction can have a deeply compromised human origin.

    Symbols are tools, not revelations

    Rojas began exploring notation because students who disengaged from abstract lessons became attentive when they learned who created the concepts and why. That response points to the article’s larger lesson: mathematics is a historical and cultural process, not a finished language discovered all at once.

    Today’s notation is powerful precisely because generations of people tested, rejected and standardized alternatives. Symbols let mathematicians communicate complex relationships with extraordinary economy, but their apparent inevitability is an illusion. Behind every compact mark lies a record of practical needs, printing technology, intellectual competition and lives that were as complicated as those in any other part of history.
